## 1. UK financial promotions to professional fund buyers: FSMA s21, the FPO, COBS 4, past performance, and what "for professional investors only" plus professional-only targeting buys you

### Takeaway
- The manager is FCA-authorised, so its own promotions meet s21 automatically. What actually binds B2B digital promotions is:
  - COBS 4 (fair, clear and not misleading; MiFID Article 44 standards, which reach professional clients too; the past-performance conditions);
  - the Principles, Consumer Duty and the anti-greenwashing rule.
- A "for professional investors only" label is necessary but not enough. The promotion has to be directed only at investment professionals in fact. On open social platforms that takes:
  - layered targeting;
  - a gated landing page;
  - kept evidence of who was reached;
  - creative that would do no harm if a retail investor saw it.

### Cited Findings
- **FSMA s21.**
  - s21(1) bars any person, in the course of business, from communicating an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity.
  - s21(2) disapplies this where the communicator is an authorised person, or where the content has been approved by an authorised person.
  - s21(3): a communication originating outside the UK is caught only if it is capable of having an effect in the UK.
  - Source: [FSMA 2000 s21](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/8/section/21) [K] (high).
- **Penalties.** Breaching s21 is a criminal offence (s25: up to 2 years' imprisonment and/or a fine), and resulting agreements may be unenforceable (s30). — [FSMA s25](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/8/section/25) [K] (high)
- **Approver "gateway" (from 7 February 2024).** An authorised firm that approves financial promotions for unauthorised persons needs FCA approver permission, subject to exemptions. The exemptions include:
  - approving promotions of unauthorised members of its own group;
  - approving its own promotions that unauthorised persons then communicate.
  - Source: [FCA PS23/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/policy-statements/ps23-13-introducing-gateway-firms-approving-financial-promotions) [K] (high on the date; medium on the exact exemption wording).
- **FPO 2005 Art 19 (investment professionals).**
  - s21 does not apply to a communication made only to recipients the communicator reasonably believes to be investment professionals, or that may reasonably be regarded as directed only at them.
  - It is treated as so directed if all three conditions are met:
    - (a) it states it is directed at persons with professional experience and that the investment is available only to them;
    - (b) it states that persons without such experience should not rely on it;
    - (c) proper systems and procedures stop other recipients engaging in the investment activity with the communicator or its group.
  - Meeting only some of the conditions counts as a factor, not a safe harbour.
  - "Investment professional" (Art 19(5)) covers:
    - authorised persons;
    - exempt persons, such as appointed representatives, for the activities they are exempt for;
    - persons whose ordinary business involves the activity;
    - directors, officers and employees of those persons who receive the communication in that capacity and whose responsibilities involve the activity.
  - Source: [FPO 2005 art 19](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1529/article/19) [K] (high).
- **FPO Art 12.** Communications made to, or directed only at, persons outside the UK are exempt from s21. This is relevant when the UK team targets EU or Swiss audiences. — [FPO art 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1529/article/12) [K] (high that it exists; medium on its conditions)
- **COBS 4.2.1R.** A firm must ensure that a communication or financial promotion is fair, clear and not misleading.
  - The rule is drafted with carve-outs for some promotions to professionals in non-MiFID business. These use the Glossary concepts "excluded communication" (for example, a promotion that would be exempt under the FPO if an unauthorised person made it) and "non-retail communication".
  - For MiFID business, MiFID II Art 24(3) requires all information to clients, including marketing communications, to be fair, clear and not misleading, and requires marketing communications to be clearly identifiable.
  - Sources: [COBS 4.2](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/2.html); [MiFID II](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2014/65/oj) [K] (high on the fair, clear and not misleading standard; medium on the carve-out mechanics, which need verifying against the COBS 4.1/4.2 text).
- **MiFID Org Regulation Art 44.** It is retained in UK law and reproduced in COBS 4.5A.
  - It applies to information "addressed to, or disseminated in such a way that it is likely to be received by, retail clients or potential retail clients or professional clients or potential professional clients, including marketing communications".
  - Requirements:
    - the firm is named;
    - the information is accurate;
    - risks get a fair and prominent indication whenever benefits are mentioned, with risk text in a font at least the size of the predominant font;
    - it is understandable by the average member of the target group;
    - warnings are not disguised;
    - it is up to date and suited to the medium.
  - Sources: [Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/565](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_del/2017/565/oj); UK copy in [COBS 4.5A](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/5A.html) [K] (high on the EU text; medium on where it now sits in the UK Handbook after the Smarter Regulatory Framework restatement).
- **Past-performance conditions** (Art 44(4); COBS 4.6.2R for non-MiFID retail communications).
  - Past performance must not be the most prominent feature.
  - It must cover the preceding 5 years (or the whole period, if shorter) in complete 12-month periods.
  - The reference period and source must be stated.
  - It must carry a prominent warning that past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results.
  - A currency warning is needed where figures are in another currency.
  - The effect of fees must be disclosed where performance is shown gross.
  - Simulated past performance and future-performance forecasts have parallel conditions: reasonable assumptions, positive and negative scenarios, and a warning (Art 44(5)–(6); COBS 4.6.6R–4.6.7R).
  - Sources: [COBS 4.6](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/6.html); [2017/565](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_del/2017/565/oj) [K] (high on content; medium on the COBS paragraph numbers).
- **UK UCITS promotions** must say a prospectus exists and the key investor information is available, say where to get them, and not contradict them (COBS 4.13, implementing UCITS Art 77). — [COBS 4.13](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/13.html) [K] (medium)
- **Records.** Firms must keep records of the financial promotions they communicate or approve (COBS 4.11). Retention is longer for MiFID business. — [COBS 4.11](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/11.html) [K] (medium on the retention periods)
- **FCA Register data.** The FCA Register API is built for single look-ups and is rate-limited. Bulk data comes through the paid Register Extract Service (subscriber handbook dated April 2026). This matters when building an auditable professional-only account list. — [FCA Register Extract Service handbook](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/documents/register-extract-handbook.pdf) [S]

### Inferences
- **Who needs what.**
  - The UK manager's own promotions of UK OEICs, and of Irish/Lux UCITS to UK professionals, run under COBS 4, the Principles, PRIN 2A and ESG 4.3.1R.
  - FPO Art 19 starts to matter when an unauthorised person communicates. Examples: a paid creator, podcast host or conference organiser, or an EU ManCo or US affiliate with no UK authorisation.
  - Under the gateway exemptions:
    - the manager can approve its own promotion for an unauthorised person to carry without approver permission;
    - another group entity's promotion relies on the group exemption;
    - approving a third party's own content (for example, a creator's independent commentary) needs approver permission.
- **What the label does and does not cover.**
  - A "professional investors only" label meets FPO Art 19(4)(a)–(b).
  - Condition (c), stopping non-professionals transacting with the communicator or its group, is hard to meet for UK OEICs that retail investors can buy on platforms. So the "deemed directed" safe harbour may not be available.
  - The manager then has to rely on the overall "may reasonably be regarded as directed only at" test. The same evidence decides whether the promotion is a "non-retail communication" under COBS.
  - Targeting by job title is therefore supporting evidence, not a safe harbour. LinkedIn profile data is self-declared, and posts can be shared beyond the target audience.
- **"Reasonable steps" for LinkedIn, Microsoft or programmatic B2B fund-specific promotions:**
  - (a) Build the audience from authoritative registers (FCA RES extract, FINMA, ORIAS/AMF, OCF, CNMV, DIHK). Layer job function and seniority on top. Exclude students, retired and unemployed members, own staff and competitors.
  - (b) Switch off audience expansion, lookalike/predictive audiences and off-platform networks (e.g., LinkedIn Audience Network) for fund-specific creative.
  - (c) Put the professional-only statement and a capital-at-risk statement inside the creative itself, not only in the caption (FG24/1's standalone-compliance test; see section 2).
  - (d) Use a professional-investor gate on the landing page: country plus investor-type self-certification, with fund lists tied to registration status. Record each acceptance.
  - (e) Keep campaign settings, delivery and demographic reports (company, function and seniority breakdowns) and approvals, so you can show who the audience was.
  - (f) Test for leakage periodically, for example from engagement, comments and follower demographics.
  - (g) Make sure the creative would not mislead or harm a retail investor who sees it anyway (Consumer Duty's "avoid foreseeable harm"; see section 3).
- **Performance in short-form ads.** Showing performance in a social or programmatic ad brings in the full Art 44(4)/COBS 4.6 set: 5 discrete 12-month periods, warnings, a currency warning (for example, a GBP class shown to EUR or CHF buyers) and fee effects.
  - The safest pattern is a performance-free ad that links to a gated factsheet or KID.
  - Any performance block must be a pre-approved template. Never use cherry-picked windows such as "since the 2020 low".
- **Variants are separate promotions.** Every A/B or dynamic variant is its own financial promotion for record-keeping (COBS 4.11) and approval.

## 2. FCA FG24/1 on social media, finfluencers, and enforcement in 2024–2026

### Takeaway
- FG24/1 treats every post, ad, image, meme or creator video as a financial promotion that must be compliant on its own. Character limits are no excuse.
- Firms are accountable for promotions they commission from creators, and those promotions need approval by an authorised person.
- The FCA has backed this with criminal prosecutions of unauthorised finfluencers. Those cases are retail-facing, but the standard carries over to B2B creator content.

### Cited Findings
- **FG24/1 "Finalised guidance on financial promotions on social media"** (published March 2024; replaced FG15/4). Its main points:
  - (i) Each promotion must be compliant on its own. A post cannot rely on a link, a thread or "see more" to supply balance or risk warnings.
  - (ii) If a format cannot carry the required information, it may be unsuitable for that promotion.
  - (iii) Risk warnings must be prominent within the post, image or video.
  - (iv) The "image advertisement" concept is narrow: essentially name, logo, contact point and the type of regulated activity.
  - (v) Memes and informal formats count as promotions if they invite or induce, and must not trivialise risk.
  - (vi) Firms using influencers or affiliates must approve the content, do due diligence and monitor. An unauthorised person who communicates an unapproved promotion may commit a criminal offence.
  - (vii) Consumer Duty applies.
  - Source: [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/finalised-guidance/fg24-1-finalised-guidance-financial-promotions-social-media) [K] (medium-high on these themes; paragraph numbers not verified).
- **ASA/CAP.** The rules on making clear that ads are ads (an "Ad"/"#ad" label upfront) apply to influencer content alongside FCA rules. — [CAP/ASA influencers' guide](https://www.asa.org.uk/resource/influencers-guide.html) [K] (medium)
- **LinkedIn Thought Leader Ads.**
  - A company can sponsor posts by employees or other members, who must approve each request.
  - The ad runs the original post unedited and carries a "promoted" tag.
  - Only certain post types and objectives are eligible.
  - Source: [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/thought-leader-ads); [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-enable-brands-sponsor-any-organic-post-app/710255/) [S] (secondary).

### Inferences
- **FG24/1 in a B2B setting.** FG24/1 is written mainly for consumer-facing promotions, but its tests apply to any financial promotion on social media, including LinkedIn. For a professional-only campaign, each creative should carry within itself:
  - the professional-only statement;
  - the firm's identity;
  - balanced risk messaging;
  - either full Art 44(4) performance data or none.
- **Thought Leader Ads.** Because they cannot be edited, the manager should sponsor only posts it has pre-agreed and approved. A sponsored post becomes the firm's promotion.
- **Employee advocacy.** Portfolio managers' reposts and comments made in their employment capacity can be firm promotions. This needs a social-media policy, pre-approved templates, archiving, and a ban on direct messages that amount to unsolicited real-time promotions.

### Gaps
- **Enforcement record not verified today.** From prior knowledge, needing confirmation in the FCA press archive (search unavailable):
  - June 2024: the FCA charged nine individuals, including reality-TV personalities, with s21 offences for promoting an unauthorised FX trading scheme on Instagram.
  - 2025: guilty pleas and sentences in some of those cases.
  - June 2025: an FCA-led international "week of action" against finfluencers with other regulators, involving hundreds of takedown requests and interviews under caution.
  - Any 2026 cases and the FCA's 2025 financial-promotions intervention statistics were not retrieved.
- No source found on whether the FCA has commented specifically on B2B or professional-audience creators.

## 3. Does Consumer Duty reach a manufacturer's wholesale communications?

### Takeaway
- The Duty does not apply to dealings with professional clients as such.
- A UK manufacturer of funds sold to retail investors is, however, within the Duty for its role in the distribution chain. B2B communications to advisers and DFMs must not foreseeably cause retail harm, and must give intermediaries what they need to explain products clearly to retail customers.

### Cited Findings
- **PRIN 2A (Consumer Duty).**
  - In force 31 July 2023 for open products and 31 July 2024 for closed books.
  - It comprises Principle 12, the cross-cutting obligations (act in good faith; avoid foreseeable harm; enable and support retail customers) and four outcomes, including consumer understanding (PRIN 2A.5).
  - "Retail customer" in investment business excludes professional clients and eligible counterparties.
  - Source: [PRIN 2A](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PRIN/2A/) [K] (high).
- **Distribution chains.** The Duty applies to firms that can determine or materially influence retail outcomes, including manufacturers with no direct customer relationship. Firms in a chain must cooperate and share information. FG22/5 explains consumer-understanding expectations, including for communications delivered through intermediaries. — [FG22/5](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/finalised-guidance/fg22-5-final-non-handbook-guidance-firms-consumer-duty) [K] (high on the principle; paragraph references not verified)
- **Market context.**
  - The FCA's 2025 advice-market data shows 5,548 advice firms (down about 15% since 2021) and about 31,000 advisers. — [FCA news, adviser survey](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/news-stories/fca-publishes-findings-financial-adviser-market-survey) [S]
  - The FCA's 2026 wealth-management survey (published 18 Aug 2026) counts about 500 investment-management firms, with the 10 largest serving up to 89% of clients. — [FCA wealth management survey report 2026](https://www.fca.org.uk/data/wealth-management-survey-report-2026) [S]

### Inferences
- **What applies to a B2B ad.**
  - A B2B ad to advisers is not a "communication with a retail customer", so the PRIN 2A.5 communication tests do not apply to the ad directly.
  - The cross-cutting obligations do apply, as does the duty to give distributors what they need.
  - In practice: keep B2B messaging consistent with the fund's target market and fair-value assessment. Do not push products outside the target market through adviser campaigns. Assume B2B creative may be forwarded to clients.
- **Institutional-only products** that are never distributed to retail fall outside the Duty.

### Gaps
- From prior knowledge, the FCA said in 2025 that it would clarify how the Duty applies across distribution chains. Whether any rule or guidance change followed by October 2026 is not verified.

## 4. Sustainability-related fund marketing: UK anti-greenwashing rule, SDR naming and marketing rules, and how they interact with EU rules

### Takeaway
- The FCA's anti-greenwashing rule (since 31 May 2024) covers every sustainability reference an FCA-authorised firm makes, including to professional clients.
- The SDR naming and marketing rules (since 2 December 2024, with flexibility to 2 April 2025) restrict ESG terms for UK funds.
- Irish/Lux UCITS fall instead under ESMA's fund-names guidelines (from 21 Nov 2024 for new funds and 21 May 2025 for existing funds). The UK firm's own communications about those funds remain under the anti-greenwashing rule.

### Cited Findings
- **ESG 4.3.1R (anti-greenwashing rule).**
  - Any reference to the sustainability characteristics of a product or service must be consistent with those characteristics and fair, clear and not misleading.
  - It applies when communicating with clients in the UK about a product or service, and when communicating or approving a financial promotion to a person in the UK.
  - In force since 31 May 2024.
  - Source: [ESG 4.3](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/ESG/4/3.html) [K] (high).
- **FG24/3 (April 2024).** Sustainability claims must be:
  - correct and capable of substantiation;
  - clear and presented so they can be understood;
  - complete, not omitting or hiding important information;
  - fair and meaningful in any comparison.
  - The guidance covers images, visuals and sounds as well as words.
  - Source: [FG24/3](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/finalised-guidance/fg24-3-finalised-non-handbook-guidance-anti-greenwashing-rule) [K] (high).
- **PS23/16 (28 Nov 2023), the SDR.**
  - Labels available from 31 July 2024; naming and marketing rules from 2 December 2024.
  - Rules for unlabelled UK funds:
    - "sustainable", "sustainability" and "impact" may not be used in the name;
    - other sustainability terms are allowed only with conditions, including disclosures and a statement that the product has no label.
  - Scope is UK funds and portfolio products managed by UK firms; overseas funds are not in scope.
  - Source: [PS23/16](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/policy-statements/ps23-16-sustainability-disclosure-requirements-investment-labels) [K] (high on dates; medium on term-level detail).
- **Temporary flexibility.** The FCA gave firms that had applied for a label and were awaiting a decision until 2 April 2025 to comply with the naming and marketing rules. — [FCA SDR and labelling regime page](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/climate-change-sustainable-finance/sustainability-disclosure-and-labelling-regime) [K] (medium)
- **EU Directive (EU) 2024/825 (Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition).** It amends the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive to ban generic environmental claims and uncertified sustainability labels in consumer-facing commercial practices. It applies from 27 September 2026, and only to consumer-facing (B2C) practices. — [Directive (EU) 2024/825](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/825/oj) [K] (medium-high)
- ESMA's fund-names guidelines are covered in section 6, and France's AMF DOC-2020-03 in section 7.

### Inferences
- **ESG ads to UK advisers.** B2B ESG ads to UK advisers fall under the anti-greenwashing rule even when the audience is professional, because advisers are clients or recipients of promotions in the UK. Green imagery (forests, wind turbines) on a fund with no ESG characteristics is a classic FG24/3 risk.
- **UK OEICs with ESG terms.** Every marketing asset, social ads included, must meet the ESG 4.3 naming and marketing conditions or use the label. Avoid "sustainable" and "impact" in marketing for unlabelled products.
- **Irish/Lux UCITS.**
  - The SDR naming and marketing rules do not apply, but the anti-greenwashing rule does for UK communications, and ESMA's names guidelines govern the fund name across the EU.
  - Keep a single claims library that satisfies the anti-greenwashing rule, SFDR disclosures and ESMA's sustainability guidance on marketing communications (section 6).
- **EU public-facing ads after 27 Sept 2026.** Avoid unsubstantiated generic green claims in any EU ad that consumers could see, even if aimed at professionals, because the green-transition rules apply from that date.

### Gaps
- Not verified whether the FCA extended SDR to portfolio management or overseas funds (the CP24/8 follow-up), or whether it requires distributors to flag that overseas funds are unlabelled.
- From prior knowledge, the Commission proposed an SFDR review in November 2025 with product categories and limits on ESG terms in names and marketing for uncategorised products. Its status in October 2026 is not verified.

## 5. Inducements: paying platforms, distributors, networks or creators for marketing, data, CPD and hospitality

### Takeaway
- Paying a distributor for a genuine service (ad inventory, data, event costs) can work. It must be an arm's-length payment for a real service, and it must be documented.
- Payments or benefits that could influence a firm advising clients or managing portfolios are banned for independent advice and portfolio management, apart from minor non-monetary benefits. This follows from MiFID II Art 24(7)–(8) and, in the UK, COBS 2.3A and the adviser-charging rules.
- UK platforms are under the COBS 6.1E payment rules.
- Paying "adviser-influencers", or paying for prominence on a fund list, is red.

### Cited Findings
- **MiFID II Art 24(7)(b), 24(8) and 24(9).**
  - Firms providing independent advice or portfolio management must not accept and retain fees, commissions or any monetary or non-monetary benefit from third parties.
  - The exception is clearly disclosed minor non-monetary benefits that can enhance service quality and do not impair the duty to act in the client's best interest.
  - Under Art 24(9), any other inducement is allowed only if it is designed to enhance quality, does not impair that duty, and is disclosed.
  - Source: [MiFID II](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2014/65/oj) [K] (high).
- **Delegated Directive (EU) 2017/593.** Art 11 sets the quality-enhancement criteria. Art 12(3) lists acceptable minor non-monetary benefits:
  - generic or personalised information or documentation about an instrument or service;
  - issuer-paid material on a new issuance;
  - participation in conferences, seminars and other training events on the benefits and features of a specific financial instrument or investment service;
  - hospitality of a reasonable de minimis value, such as food and drink at a business meeting, conference or training event.
  - These benefits must be reasonable, proportionate and unlikely to influence the recipient's behaviour to the client's detriment.
  - Source: [Delegated Directive 2017/593](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir_del/2017/593/oj) [K] (high on the core list).
- **UK rules.**
  - COBS 2.3A carries the MiFID inducement rules; the UK also applies the ban to restricted advice to retail clients.
  - The adviser-charging rules in COBS 6.1A ban adviser commission on retail investment products.
  - FCA FG14/1 (2014) gave guidance on provider payments and benefits to advisers (training, conferences, marketing support, hospitality) as potential inducements.
  - Sources: [COBS 2.3A](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/2/3A.html); [COBS 6.1A](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/6/1A.html) [K] (medium on detail).
- **UK platform payments (PS13/1, 2013; now COBS 6.1E).**
  - Platforms are paid through platform charges agreed with the client.
  - Cash rebates above £1 per month per fund were banned on non-advised platforms.
  - Limited provider payments remain allowed, for example for advertising products on the platform or for producing management information, provided they do not vary inappropriately and reflect legitimate costs.
  - Sources: [FCA PS13/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/policy-statements/ps13-1-payments-platform-service-providers-and-cash-rebates-providers); [Pinsent Masons Out-Law guide](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/guides/-platforms-and-remuneration) [S] (secondary summary; the current COBS 6.1E text was not re-checked).
- **Switzerland, FinSA Art 26.** A financial service provider may accept third-party remuneration only if it tells clients in advance and they expressly waive it, or if it passes the remuneration on in full. — [FinSA](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2019/758/en) [K] (high)
- **Examples of paid "ecosystem" services.**
  - Dynamic Planner partners with 150+ asset managers to risk-profile 1,700+ funds and model portfolios. — [Dynamic Planner, asset managers](https://dynamicplanner.com/asset-managers/) [S]
  - Candriam Academy offers free accredited CPD (accredited by EFPA France) and had 16,400 members in 2024. — [Candriam CSR report 2025](https://csr.candriam.com/pdf/Candriam_2025_CSR%20Report-pages-Citizens.pdf) [S]

### Inferences
Inducement risk by type of sponsorship:
- **Paid inventory on distributor-owned media** (platform newsletters, adviser-platform banners, sponsored platform webinars): amber.
  - UK platforms: allowed if it is a market-rate payment for advertising that is not tied to flows and does not influence "best buy" lists, model portfolios or fund lists.
  - EU distributors serving clients (banks, B2B fund platforms that are MiFID firms or banks): treat as a third-party payment that must pass the Art 24(9) tests and be disclosed, unless it is clearly a fee for a separate service at market value. Document the assessment.
- **Buying data or MI from distributors** (flows, adviser engagement analytics): amber, usually workable. Keep pricing flat or at market rates, never volume-linked, and keep a written services agreement.
- **Sponsoring distributor events or CPD:** amber.
  - The adviser's participation in product-specific training, plus de minimis food and drink, fits the minor-benefit list.
  - A sponsorship fee paid to the distributor is a monetary benefit. For independent advisers and portfolio managers it should be limited to cost-proportionate contributions.
  - Generic CPD (for example, tax technicalities) is not on the EU list, so it is riskier for independent recipients.
  - Avoid paying travel or accommodation.
- **Paying creators who practise as advisers or discretionary managers:** red. It is a benefit to a firm under the UK adviser-charging/RDR bans and the MiFID bans. Pay only creators with no client-facing advice or management role, such as genuine media, and treat the content as the manager's promotion.
- **Paying for "preferred", "recommended" or fund-list placement:** red in the UK (COBS 6.1E and RDR logic) and in the EU wherever the recipient advises or manages portfolios.
- **Switzerland:** sponsorship of events run by independent asset managers is a benefit to them. Structure it as payment for services and expect FinSA Art 26 disclosure or pass-through.

### Gaps
- Not verified: the current COBS 6.1E text (including any rule against preferential treatment) and any 2025–26 FCA work to simplify inducement rules.
- Not verified: ESMA Q&As on event-sponsorship and hosting-cost payments.
- **EU Retail Investment Strategy.** The Commission's May 2023 proposal included a partial inducement ban, a value-for-money test and new MiFID rules on marketing communications and finfluencers (COM(2023) 279, [proposal](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52023PC0279) [K]). The trilogue outcome and its application dates in October 2026 are not verified.
- The national total inducement bans (for example the Netherlands) were not re-checked.

## 6. EU fund-marketing framework: CBDF Regulation and Directive, ESMA marketing guidelines, pre-marketing, de-notification, ESMA fund-names guidelines

### Takeaway
- For Irish/Lux UCITS, the EU ManCo is legally responsible under Art 4 of the CBDF Regulation for every marketing communication, including social posts, banners and videos. Each must:
  - be identifiable as marketing;
  - give risks and rewards equal prominence;
  - carry ESMA's disclaimer and the prospectus/KID references;
  - stay consistent with the legal documents.
- Pre-marketing exists only for EU AIFMs. It cannot be used for UCITS or by a UK firm.
- UK OEICs count as non-EU AIFs in the EU, so marketing them there means national private placement, generally to professional investors only.

### Cited Findings
- **Regulation (EU) 2019/1156 (CBDF Regulation).**
  - Art 4(1): marketing communications addressed to investors must be identifiable as such, describe risks and rewards with equal prominence, and be fair, clear and not misleading.
  - Further paragraphs:
    - communications containing specific information must not contradict or diminish the prospectus or KID;
    - they must say where and in which language those documents can be obtained;
    - they must give information on investor rights and the possibility of de-notification.
  - Applies from 2 August 2021.
  - National competent authorities may require marketing communications to be filed for ex-ante verification, though filing is not a precondition for marketing.
  - Source: [Regulation (EU) 2019/1156](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/1156/oj) [K] (high on Art 4(1); medium on the split of the later paragraphs).
- **Directive (EU) 2019/1160 (CBDF Directive; transposition 2 August 2021).**
  - Pre-marketing for EU AIFMs (AIFMD Art 30a):
    - an informal letter to the regulator within two weeks;
    - no subscription forms or final-form documents;
    - draft documents must state they are not an offer;
    - subscriptions by professional investors within 18 months are deemed to result from marketing;
    - only authorised entities may pre-market on the AIFM's behalf.
  - De-notification (UCITS Art 93a; AIFMD Art 32a):
    - a blanket redemption offer open for at least 30 working days;
    - a public notice;
    - distribution arrangements with intermediaries terminated;
    - after de-notification, no new direct or indirect offering or placement;
    - AIFMs may not pre-market the same or similar strategies in that state for 36 months.
  - Local physical facilities are no longer required (UCITS Art 92).
  - Source: [Directive (EU) 2019/1160](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/1160/oj) [K] (high on the regime; medium on details).
- **ESMA34-45-1272, Guidelines on marketing communications.**
  - Published in all EU languages 2 August 2021; apply from 2 February 2022.
  - They cover:
    - identification, with a prescribed disclaimer: "This is a marketing communication. Please refer to the [prospectus …] and to the [KID] before making any final investment decisions";
    - equal prominence of risks and rewards;
    - consistency with legal and regulatory documents;
    - costs;
    - past performance (consistent with the KIID/KID presentation and not the most prominent feature);
    - expected future performance (based on reasonable, objective assumptions);
    - sustainability (consistent with SFDR disclosures, and not disproportionate to how far the strategy actually integrates sustainability).
  - They apply across media, including online and social media.
  - Source: [ESMA34-45-1272](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/esma34-45-1272_guidelines_on_marketing_communications.pdf) [K] (high on dates and headings; medium on the social-media wording).
- **ESMA Guidelines on fund names using ESG or sustainability terms (ESMA34-1592494965-657).**
  - Final report 14 May 2024; all languages 21 August 2024; apply from 21 November 2024 to new funds and from 21 May 2025 to existing funds.
  - Core rules:
    - at least 80% of investments must meet the environmental/social characteristics or sustainable-investment objectives;
    - environmental, impact and sustainability terms trigger the Paris-aligned Benchmark exclusions (Delegated Reg 2020/1818 Art 12(1)(a)–(g));
    - transition, social and governance terms trigger the Climate Transition Benchmark exclusions (Art 12(1)(a)–(c));
    - "sustainable" terms require meaningful investment in sustainable investments as defined in SFDR Art 2(17);
    - "transition" and "impact" terms need a clear, measurable path or impact.
  - Source: [ESMA34-1592494965-657](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2024-08/ESMA34-1592494965-657_Guidelines_on_funds_names_using_ESG_or_sustainability_related_terms.pdf) [K] (high).
- **AIFMD II (Directive (EU) 2024/927).**
  - In force 15 April 2024; transposition due by 16 April 2026.
  - It tightens the conditions for national private placement of non-EU AIFs and AIFMs. For example, the third country must not be on the EU anti-money-laundering high-risk list or the EU list of non-cooperative tax jurisdictions, and tax information-exchange arrangements must be in place.
  - It also amends the delegation and liquidity-tool rules in both AIFMD and the UCITS Directive.
  - Source: [Directive (EU) 2024/927](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/927/oj) [K] (high on dates; medium on NPPR detail).
- **UK OEICs in the EU.** Since 1 January 2021, UK UCITS (OEICs) are non-EU AIFs in the EU. Marketing them requires national private placement under AIFMD Art 42 in each member state, generally to professional investors only. — [AIFMD](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2011/61/oj) [K] (high)

### Inferences
- **ManCo controls UCITS marketing.** For Irish/Lux UCITS, the UK distribution team's digital assets must go through the ManCo's marketing-communications policy and approval. Each post, banner, video or dynamic variant is a marketing communication. A practical pattern:
  - the disclaimer as an overlay in the image or video plus the caption;
  - the KID/prospectus link and investor-rights summary on the landing page;
  - the records the ManCo needs to answer any regulator request for ex-ante filing.
- **No "testing the waters" for UCITS.** Pre-marketing is unavailable for UCITS, so digital campaigns gauging interest in an unlaunched UCITS are marketing.
- **UK OEICs into the EU:**
  - no national private placement notification in a country means advertising there is unlawful marketing (red);
  - where notified, campaigns must exclude retail audiences, which links to the audience-status issues in section 13.

### Gaps
- Not verified:
  - ESMA Q&As on the CBDF rules (for example, whether material addressed only to distributors is in scope, and any shortened disclaimer for space-limited formats);
  - the findings of ESMA's 2023 common supervisory action on marketing communications;
  - which regulators currently require ex-ante filing of marketing communications.
- From prior knowledge only: the Commission's December 2025 market-integration package may propose changes to cross-border fund distribution. Its status is unknown.

## 7. National marketing-communication rules and positions (France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Luxembourg, Ireland)

### Takeaway
National overlays matter most in four areas:
- retail-facing material;
- ESG communication (France);
- B2B email, cookies and enforcement by competitors (Germany, Italy, Spain);
- influencer regimes (France, Italy, Spain; see section 12).

For professional-only digital campaigns, the sharpest national differences are electronic-communications and privacy rules, and how strictly regulators treat "professional-only" marketing of funds not registered for retail.

### Cited Findings
- **France: AMF DOC-2020-03** ("information to be provided by collective investment schemes incorporating non-financial approaches"; first issued 2020 and updated several times).
  - It sets when a fund may make ESG a central element of its communication (high, measurable standards), may communicate on ESG only to a limited extent, or must not feature it at all.
  - It applies to French funds and to foreign funds marketed to retail investors in France.
  - Source: [AMF DOC-2020-03](https://www.amf-france.org/en/regulation/policy/doc-2020-03) [K] (medium; the current thresholds and the 2024–25 alignment with ESMA's names guidelines are not verified).
- **France: B2B email (CNIL).** Email prospecting of professionals without prior consent is allowed if the message relates to the recipient's profession and the recipient can object. Consumer (B2C) email needs consent under CPCE L.34-5.
  - Sources: [CNIL, email prospecting](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/la-prospection-commerciale-par-courrier-electronique) [K] (medium-high); secondary comparisons agree ([Stacked Creator](https://thestackedcreator.com/articles/cold-email-gdpr/) [S]).
- **France: CNIL enforcement.** The CNIL publishes its sanctions centrally. These include large cookie and advertising fines and sanctions against B2B contact-data enrichment. — [CNIL sanctions list](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/les-sanctions-prononcees-par-la-cnil) [K] (medium; specific decisions listed under Gaps)
- **Germany: UWG §7.**
  - §7(2) No. 2: email advertising without the addressee's prior express consent is an unacceptable nuisance. It applies to B2B as well as B2C.
  - §7(3): narrow existing-customer exception (own similar goods or services, address obtained in a sale, no objection, a clear right to object at every use).
  - Sources: [UWG §7](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/uwg_2004/__7.html) [K] (high); secondary sources agree Germany is the strictest ([Stacked Creator](https://thestackedcreator.com/articles/cold-email-gdpr/) [S]).
- **Germany: UWG §5a(4).** A commercial purpose must be disclosed unless it is obvious from context. This is the basis for labelling paid influencer content "Werbung" or "Anzeige". — [UWG §5a](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/uwg_2004/__5a.html) [K] (high)
- **Germany: TDDDG §25** (formerly TTDSG, renamed May 2024). Storing or accessing information on a device needs consent unless strictly necessary. — [TDDDG §25](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ttdsg/__25.html) [K] (high)
- **Germany: KAGB §302** governs advertising for retail funds (identifiable, not misleading) and lets BaFin prohibit advertising. — [KAGB §302](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/kagb/__302.html) [K] (medium)
- **Germany: competitor lawsuits.** In C-21/23 Lindenapotheke (4 Oct 2024) the CJEU held that competitors may sue under unfair-competition law over GDPR breaches. This raises the risk of competitor or association actions in Germany over tracking and email. — [CJEU C-21/23](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62023CJ0021) [K] (high)
- **Germany: §34f GewO intermediaries.** 41,388 Finanzanlagenvermittler held a licence under §34f GewO at 1 July 2026, with about 375 fee-based advisers under §34h. They are licensed under trade law, not banking or investment-firm law (see section 13 on what that means for client status). — [DIHK register statistics](https://www.dihk.de/de/serviceportal/fuer-gewerbetreibende-unternehmer/statistiken-und-bestandszahlen-zum-vermittlerregister-141724) [S]
- **Italy: Privacy Code Art 130.** Marketing by automated calls, fax, email or SMS needs consent, with a soft opt-in for existing customers' email (Art 130(4)). Secondary sources disagree on whether the Garante tolerates B2B cold email on a legitimate-interests basis. — [Codice Privacy](https://www.garanteprivacy.it/codice) [K] (medium); [Stacked Creator](https://thestackedcreator.com/articles/cold-email-gdpr/) [S] (contested)
- **Spain: LSSI Art 21.** Unsolicited commercial email that the recipient has not requested or expressly authorised is prohibited (existing-customer exception in Art 21(2)). Secondary sources disagree on whether B2B can rely on legitimate interests. — [LSSI, Ley 34/2002](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2002-13758) [K] (medium-high); [Stacked Creator](https://thestackedcreator.com/articles/cold-email-gdpr/) [S]
- **Spain: advisers.** There were 88 EAFs (MiFID investment firms) at end-2024, down 55 because firms converted to the new national advisory firms (EAFN), of which there were 52. — [CNMV press release on 2024 annual report](https://www.cnmv.es/webservices/verdocumento/ver?e=ZBKbShY+9GuHqrBgZVA71KEpI+iFfPy6xtr5zzoQYHmkKR5xrcqgTLzGLccEARss) [S]
- **France: CIFs.** The AMF published key figures on CIFs for 2024 in December 2025. CNCGP and ANACOFI-CIF each account for close to 40% of the profession. — [AMF chiffres clés CIF 2024](https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/private/2025-12/chiffres-cles-cif-2024.pdf) [S]
- **Ireland: B2B email.** The ePrivacy Regulations (S.I. 336/2011) Reg 13 require consent for marketing email to individuals and set a different regime for non-individual subscribers. — [S.I. 336/2011](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/2011/si/336/made/en/print) [K] (medium)
- **Ireland: DPC fine on LinkedIn.** The Data Protection Commission, LinkedIn's lead EU supervisor, fined LinkedIn €310m on 24 Oct 2024 for processing members' data for behavioural analysis and targeted ads without a valid legal basis. — [DPC press release](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/news-media/press-releases/irish-data-protection-commission-fines-linkedin-ireland-eu310-million) [K] (high on the fact; medium on the URL)
- **Ireland: Consumer Protection Code.** The Central Bank's revised Consumer Protection Code took effect on 24 March 2026, covering regulated entities' dealings with consumers. — [CBI Consumer Protection Code](https://www.centralbank.ie/regulation/consumer-protection/consumer-protection-code) [K] (medium)

### Inferences
- **France.** For funds marketed to French retail investors, check ESG-centric messaging against DOC-2020-03 even in B2B campaigns to CGPs, because CGP material is routinely passed on to clients. French-language versions are expected for any retail-facing material.
- **Germany.**
  - It is the hardest market for B2B email, website tracking, visitor identification and list-matching. Base German campaigns on platform-native targeting with on-platform engagement, not CRM email or pixels, unless you have consent.
  - Competitor and association enforcement (Abmahnungen) is a real cost driver.
- **Italy and Spain.** Treat B2B cold email as needing consent unless local counsel advises otherwise.
- **Luxembourg and Ireland.** As ManCo home states, they matter mainly through the ManCo's approval processes and through each regulator's adoption of ESMA's guidelines. The DPC's LinkedIn decision also shows that platform-side legal bases for EU ad targeting are fragile. Avoid tactics that depend on deep profiling inside the platform.

### Gaps
- **Spain, CNMV Circular 2/2020** (28 Oct 2020) on advertising investment products and services. From prior knowledge, unverified: it covers advertising aimed at investors in Spain across digital channels, requires internal advertising-control procedures and records, and lets the CNMV demand cessation or rectification. It may exclude advertising aimed exclusively at professional clients. Its exact scope, exclusions and any 2024–26 amendments (for example on influencers) need checking.
- **Italy, CONSOB.** Rules on advertising of offers (Issuers' Regulation), the Intermediaries Regulation, and CONSOB statements on finfluencers were not verified.
- **Luxembourg.** The CSSF circular numbers adopting ESMA's marketing and fund-names guidelines, and Luxembourg's B2B email rule for legal persons (Law of 30 May 2005), were not verified.
- **CNIL decisions, from prior knowledge and unverified:**
  - KASPR, a contact-enrichment browser extension harvesting LinkedIn contact data, fined €240,000 in December 2024;
  - Google fined €325m and Shein €150m in September 2025 over advertising and cookies.

- BaFin's current statements on social-media and finfluencer marketing, and its circular adopting ESMA's guidelines, were not verified.

## 8. Cross-border targeting, reverse solicitation, registration status, and Switzerland

### Takeaway
- ESMA and most regulators see geo-targeted digital ads into a country as solicitation, not reverse solicitation.
- So campaigns must follow a registration matrix (fund × share class × country × investor type) and be run by, or for, the entity entitled to market there.
- Lowest risk: Irish/Lux UCITS passported by the EU ManCo.
- Highest risk: the UK firm soliciting EU investors directly, whether for its own services or for UK OEICs.
- Switzerland allows professional-only marketing of foreign funds without FINMA approval. FinSA's advertising rules apply and, for some "qualified investor" segments, a Swiss representative and paying agent are needed.

### Cited Findings
- **ESMA statement on reverse solicitation** (ESMA35-43-2509, 13 January 2021).
  - Reverse solicitation must be at the client's own exclusive initiative and is read narrowly.
  - Every communication means counts when judging solicitation: press releases, internet advertising, brochures, calls, meetings.
  - Solicitation by anyone acting for the firm, or by entities with close links to it, counts.
  - Contract clauses or pop-ups asserting that the client took the initiative do not establish it.
  - The exemption does not let the firm market new categories of products or services to that client.
  - Source: [ESMA35-43-2509](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/esma35-43-2509_statement_on_reverse_solicitation.pdf) [K] (high).
- **The legislative exemptions.** MiFID II Art 42 (retail and elective professional clients) and MiFIR Art 46(5) (per se professionals and eligible counterparties) contain the reverse-solicitation exemption for third-country firms. Otherwise a national regime or authorisation is needed. — [MiFID II](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2014/65/oj); [MiFIR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2014/600/oj) [K] (high)
- **AIFMD "marketing".** AIFMD Art 4(1)(x) defines marketing as a direct or indirect offering or placement at the initiative of, or on behalf of, the AIFM to investors domiciled in the Union. Passive enquiries fall outside, but member states interpret this differently. — [AIFMD](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2011/61/oj) [K] (high)
- **UCITS notification.** UCITS marketing in another member state needs a notification under UCITS Art 93, covering the share classes concerned. — [UCITS Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2009/65/oj) [K] (high)
- **Switzerland: FinSA Art 68.**
  - Advertising must be clearly identifiable as such.
  - It must refer to the prospectus and KID and say where they can be obtained.
  - Advertising and other investor information must match them.
  - The FinSO definition of advertising covers investor-directed communications drawing attention to particular financial services or instruments.
  - Sources: [FinSA](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2019/758/en); [FinSO](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2019/763/en) [K] (high on Art 68; medium on the FinSO article).
- **Switzerland: client segments (FinSA Arts 4–5).**
  - Per se professional clients include:
    - financial intermediaries under the Banking Act, FinIA and CISA (including FINMA-licensed portfolio managers);
    - insurers and foreign supervised entities;
    - central banks;
    - public entities, pension schemes and companies with professional treasury;
    - large companies;
    - private investment structures with professional treasury.
  - Institutional clients are a subset of professional clients.
  - Wealthy private clients can opt out of private-client status (Art 5) with assets of at least CHF 2m, or at least CHF 500k plus knowledge and experience.
  - Source: [FinSA](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2019/758/en) [K] (high).
- **Switzerland: CISA Art 120.**
  - Offering a foreign fund to non-qualified investors needs FINMA approval.
  - Offering only to qualified investors does not.
  - A Swiss representative and paying agent are needed when the qualified investors include opted-out wealthy clients and certain other qualified segments. They are not needed when offering exclusively to per se professional or institutional clients (exact wording to be verified).
  - Source: [CISA](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2006/822/en) [K] (medium).
- **Switzerland: client-adviser register.** Client advisers of foreign financial service providers must register in a Swiss register (FinSA Art 28). FinSO exempts prudentially supervised foreign providers that serve only professional or institutional clients in Switzerland. — [FinSA](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2019/758/en); [FinSO](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2019/763/en) [K] (medium-high)
- **Switzerland: independent asset managers.** FINMA had licensed 1,664 portfolio managers and trustees under FinIA by end-2025 (97 of which have since left supervision). — [FINMA annual report 2025, market developments](https://report.finma.ch/2025/en/market-developments/market-developments-in-the-asset-management-industry) [S]

### Inferences
- **Registration matrix as the targeting gate.**
  - Country targeting in ad platforms should mirror the notification and registration matrix exactly. LinkedIn location is profile-based, so expats and cross-border commuters leak; add landing-page country gating that shows only funds registered for the visitor's declared country and investor type.
  - Local-language creative into a country is strong evidence of solicitation.
  - A "reverse solicitation" click-through banner does not cure targeted ads.
- **UK firm versus EU ManCo.**
  - Promoting EU-notified Irish/Lux UCITS: run campaigns from or for the ManCo, under its marketing policy and with its approval (green/amber).
  - Promoting the UK firm's own services (segregated mandates), or UK OEICs, by targeting EU investors: this is third-country solicitation. It needs a national private placement notification for UK OEICs to professionals, a national third-country regime or an EU-authorised entity for services, and specialist advice per country (amber/red).
- **Switzerland.**
  - Professional-only B2B campaigns to FINMA-licensed independent asset managers, private banks and insurers are workable without FINMA fund approval, with FinSA Art 68 labelling and prospectus/KID references.
  - Opted-out wealthy individuals, family offices without professional treasury, and unlicensed advisers bring in the representative and paying-agent requirement, or full FINMA approval for retail.
  - Swiss B2B email is mass advertising under UWG Art 3(1)(o); see section 9.

### Gaps
- Not verified:
  - each target country's current national third-country regime for UK investment firms;
  - post-AIFMD II changes to each country's private-placement regime;
  - the exact current wording of the CISA representative/paying-agent exemption;
  - any 2024–26 ESMA statement on reverse solicitation for funds or MiFID.
- **By analogy, from prior knowledge and unverified:** ESMA's reverse-solicitation guidelines under MiCA (final report December 2024) treat online ads targeted at EU clients as solicitation. That includes social media, influencers, sponsorships, geo-targeting and local-language sites. These guidelines are not binding for funds but show ESMA's thinking on digital solicitation.
- **UK inbound:** the OFR timing and transitional-regime end date for Irish/Lux UCITS were not verified. They decide whether UK retail-grade promotion of those funds is possible. Professional-only promotion of unrecognised schemes relies on the PCIS Order exemptions and the COBS non-mass-market investment exemptions (to be verified).

## 9. Data protection and ad-tech law: what enables or blocks account-based, IP-based, intent, visitor identification, location and CRM-matched tactics

### Takeaway
- B2B data is personal data whenever it relates to an identifiable person: a named adviser, a sole trader, or someone at a micro-firm.
- The consent rule for device storage and access (PECR reg 6 and ePrivacy Art 5(3)) is the main blocker for pixel retargeting, visitor identification and intent data. The UK's new Data (Use and Access) Act analytics exemption does not cover advertising.
- Platform rules tighten things further: Google's EEA consent signals, LinkedIn's EU limits, and DMA consent requirements.
- **Lowest-risk precision route:** account-based targeting on the platforms' own professional data (LinkedIn company or job targeting).
- **Highest risk:** person-level enrichment, IP-to-person resolution, precise geofencing, and cold email in Germany, Italy, Spain and Switzerland.

### Cited Findings
**UK**
- **PECR corporate versus individual subscribers.**
  - Corporate subscribers are companies, LLPs and Scottish partnerships. Individual subscribers are sole traders and other partnerships.
  - PECR's email consent rule does not apply to corporate subscribers, but UK GDPR still applies to named individuals' work emails.
  - Sole traders and partnerships need consent or the soft opt-in (existing customers only), so bought lists cannot be used for them.
  - Sources: [ICO, business-to-business marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/); [ICO, choosing a lawful basis](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/sending-direct-marketing-choosing-your-lawful-basis/) [S].
- **PECR reg 23.** Every marketing email must identify the sender and give a valid opt-out address, including emails to corporate subscribers. — [PECR reg 23](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/2426/regulation/23) [K] (high)
- **Data (Use and Access) Act 2025** (Royal Assent 19 June 2025).
  - It adds wording to UK GDPR confirming that direct marketing may be a legitimate interest.
  - It raises PECR maximum fines to UK GDPR levels (£17.5m or 4% of global turnover); secondary sources report this took effect in February 2026.
  - It adds a charity soft opt-in.
  - It adds PECR reg 6 exemptions for storage or access used solely for statistical analytics to improve a service (with clear information and an opt-out), for appearance or functionality preferences, for security and fraud prevention, and for emergency assistance.
  - Advertising and retargeting cookies stay consent-based.
  - Sources: [DUAA 2025](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/contents) [K] (high on Royal Assent; medium on exemption detail); [Global Law Experts summary](https://globallawexperts.com/b2b-email-marketing-rules-uk/) [S] (low-medium on the commencement date).

**EU law and case law**
- **ePrivacy Directive 2002/58.**
  - Art 5(3): storing or accessing information on a user's device needs consent, except for transmission or strictly necessary services.
  - Art 13: email marketing to natural persons needs prior consent (soft opt-in exception). Protection of non-natural persons is left to member states (Art 13(5)), which is why national B2B email rules diverge.
  - Source: [Directive 2002/58](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2002/58/oj) [K] (high).
- **Planet49** (C-673/17, 1 Oct 2019): pre-ticked boxes are not valid consent, and Art 5(3) applies whether or not the data is personal. — [CJEU C-673/17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62017CJ0673) [K] (high)
- **Breyer** (C-582/14, 19 Oct 2016): a dynamic IP address can be personal data for a website operator that has lawful means of identifying the user with data held by the ISP. — [CJEU C-582/14](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62014CJ0582) [K] (high)
- **IAB Europe** (C-604/22, 7 Mar 2024): the TCF consent string ("TC String") is personal data where it can be linked to an identifier such as an IP address, and the standard-setter can be a joint controller. — [CJEU C-604/22](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62022CJ0604) [K] (high)
- **Meta v Bundeskartellamt** (C-252/21, 4 July 2023):
  - personalised advertising is not necessary for performance of a contract;
  - legitimate interests is doubtful for extensive off-platform tracking that users would not expect;
  - consent must be freely given.
  - Source: [CJEU C-252/21](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62021CJ0252) [K] (high).
- **Joint controllership.**
  - Fashion ID (C-40/17, 29 July 2019): a site embedding a third-party plug-in or pixel is joint controller for collecting and transmitting the data.
  - Wirtschaftsakademie (C-210/16, 5 June 2018): a page administrator is joint controller with the platform.
  - Sources: [C-40/17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62017CJ0040); [C-210/16](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62016CJ0210) [K] (high).
- **KNLTB** (C-621/22, 4 Oct 2024): a purely commercial interest can be a legitimate interest, but processing must be strictly necessary and pass the balancing test. — [C-621/22](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62022CJ0621) [K] (high)
- **EDPS v SRB** (C-413/23 P, 4 Sept 2025):
  - pseudonymised data is not necessarily personal data for a recipient that cannot reasonably re-identify the person;
  - the controller's transparency duties are judged from the controller's side at the time of collection.
  - Source: [C-413/23 P](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62023CJ0413) [K] (medium-high).
- **Bindl v Commission** (T-354/22, 8 Jan 2025): the General Court awarded damages because a "Sign in with Facebook" button on a Commission site sent the user's IP address to the US without adequate safeguards. — [T-354/22](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62022TJ0354) [K] (medium-high)
- **Latombe v Commission** (T-553/23, 3 Sept 2025): the General Court dismissed the challenge to the EU-US Data Privacy Framework. An appeal may be pending. — [T-553/23](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62023TJ0553) [K] (medium-high)
- **EDPB Guidelines 2/2023** on the technical scope of Art 5(3) (final version adopted October 2024). They bring tracking pixels, link decoration, unique identifiers and IP-based tracking (where the IP address is obtained from the device) within Art 5(3). — [EDPB Guidelines 2/2023](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-22023-technical-scope-art-53-eprivacy-directive_en) [K] (medium-high)
- **EDPB Guidelines 8/2020** on targeting social media users (v2.0, April 2021).
  - Advertiser and platform are typically joint controllers.
  - Targeting with uploaded lists ("provided data") needs a valid legal basis and transparency. Legitimate interests may work only within users' reasonable expectations; otherwise consent is needed.
  - Pixel or cookie-based targeting needs consent under ePrivacy.
  - Source: [EDPB Guidelines 8/2020](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-82020-targeting-social-media-users_en) [K] (medium-high).
- **DSA (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065; fully applicable 17 Feb 2024).**
  - Art 26(1): ads must be labelled, and must show on whose behalf they run, who paid, and the main targeting parameters.
  - Art 26(2): platforms must give creators a way to declare commercial content.
  - Art 26(3): no ads based on profiling with GDPR Art 9 special-category data.
  - Art 28(2): no profiling-based ads to minors where the platform is reasonably certain the user is a minor.
  - Art 39: very large platforms must keep ad repositories.
  - Source: [DSA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj) [K] (high).
- **DMA (Regulation (EU) 2022/1925) Art 5(2).**
  - Gatekeepers may not combine or cross-use personal data across their core platform services or with third-party services, or use third-party data for ads, without GDPR consent.
  - This underpins Google's EU consent requirements, Meta's consent and "less personalised ads" model, and limits on combining LinkedIn and Microsoft data.
  - Source: [DMA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/1925/oj) [K] (high on Art 5(2)).
  - The Commission fined Meta €200m in April 2025 over its "pay or consent" model [K] (medium).

### Inferences
- **Account-based targeting on platform data** (LinkedIn company lists and job/function/seniority; Microsoft's LinkedIn-profile targeting for the UK, France and Germany):
  - The advertiser uploads company names, which are not personal data. It is joint controller for the targeting criteria under EDPB 8/2020, and transparency comes through the platform's ad explanations (DSA Art 26).
  - Avoid criteria that proxy special-category data, because of DSA Art 26(3).
  - Risk: low. This is the best "green" precision tool.
- **Contact-list matching** (LinkedIn Matched Audiences, Google Customer Match, Meta):
  - The advertiser is controller for the upload. Hashed emails remain personal data because the platform can re-identify them; EDPS v SRB does not change that.
  - UK: a legitimate-interests basis is workable for existing B2B contacts if the privacy notice names matching with ad platforms and objections (an absolute right in direct marketing) are suppressed.
  - EEA: Google effectively requires recorded consent.
  - Germany: assume consent is needed.
- **Pixel retargeting** needs consent everywhere in the UK and EU (Switzerland: inform plus opt-out).
- **Reverse-IP visitor identification:**
  - IP addresses are personal data (Breyer).
  - A JavaScript tag that reads device data needs Art 5(3)/reg 6 consent (EDPB 2/2023). The UK analytics exemption is for improving the service, not for sales prospecting.
  - Server-side IP-only logging avoids reg 6 but not UK GDPR. It still needs a legitimate-interests assessment, notice and an objection route.
  - Company-level output is still personal data for sole traders and micro-firms, which are common among IFAs, CGPs and §34f intermediaries.
  - Never resolve visits to named individuals or enrich them with contact data without an Art 14 notice. The CNIL's KASPR-type enforcement (if confirmed) shows this is policed.
  - Germany: red without consent.
- **Intent data:**
  - Prefer account-level topic signals. Diligence the vendor's sources: publisher co-op, consent strings (TCF after IAB Europe), whether personal data is delivered.
  - Use controller-to-controller terms, plus DPF/UK data bridge certification (or transfer clauses) for US vendors.
  - Person-level intent with contact data is amber/red in the EU.
- **Location and geofencing:**
  - Coarse country, region or city targeting through platforms is fine, and is needed for registration gating.
  - Precise device-level geofences (event venues, competitor or bank offices) bought from location-data brokers depend on fragile app-SDK consent. In a B2B setting, micro-geofences can single out individuals. Red in Germany and France, amber or red elsewhere.
  - Use event organisers' first-party audiences, or the platform's own radius tools, instead.
- **Clean rooms** reduce data exposure but do not remove the need for a legal basis or transparency. Insist on aggregation thresholds for outputs, set controller roles, and limit purposes.

### Gaps
- No regulator decision specifically on reverse-IP company-identification tools (Leadfeeder/Dealfront, Lead Forensics) was found. Search was unavailable; none is known from prior knowledge.

  - the B2B email rules for legal persons in Ireland and Luxembourg.

## 10. Platform policies (Google, Microsoft, Meta, LinkedIn, X, Spotify/Acast, AI assistants)

### Takeaway
Platform rules now often bite before the law does:
- Google requires financial-services verification in the UK and, since July 2026, across most of the EU/EEA.
- Microsoft requires FCA verification for UK financial-services ads.
- OpenAI restricts investing ads outside the US to select approved advertisers.
- LinkedIn has EU-specific targeting and messaging limits.

Plan verification lead times. Keep evidence of regulator authorisation for each advertising entity in each country, and decide which legal entity (UK firm or EU ManCo) advertises where.

## 11. AI-generated content: EU AI Act Article 50 (and the Omnibus), FCA, ASA/CAP, cloning a portfolio manager's voice or likeness, ESMA's AI statement

### Takeaway
- AI-generated creative is usable under existing UK and EU marketing rules, provided humans approve it and it is accurate.
- From 2 August 2026, EU AI Act Art 50 requires deployers to disclose deep fakes, such as a synthetic portfolio-manager video or voice.
- Secondary sources report the AI Omnibus (in force 27 July 2026) left Art 50's timing unchanged, apart from a marking grace period to 2 Dec 2026 for generative tools already on the market.
- Cloning a real portfolio manager needs explicit, revocable contractual consent, per-script approval and, in the EU, disclosure.

### Cited Findings
- **AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) Art 50.**
  - 50(1): providers of AI systems that interact with people must design them so users know they are dealing with AI.
  - 50(2): providers of generative systems must mark outputs in a machine-readable, detectable format.
  - 50(4): deployers of systems that generate or manipulate deep-fake image, audio or video must disclose that it is artificial. Disclosure is lighter for evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional works.
  - 50(4): deployers publishing AI-generated text to inform the public on matters of public interest must disclose it, unless there is human review or editorial control and editorial responsibility.
  - 50(5): disclosures must be clear and distinguishable at first exposure.
  - Applies from 2 August 2026.
  - Source: [AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) [K] (high).
- **Reach and penalties.** The Act covers third-country providers and deployers where the output is used in the Union (Art 2(1)(c)). Art 50 breaches carry fines up to €15m or 3% of worldwide turnover (Art 99(4)). AI literacy duties (Art 4) have applied since 2 Feb 2025. — [AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) [K] (high; check whether the Omnibus amended Art 4)
- **AI Omnibus** (secondary sources; verify in the Official Journal).
  - Reported adopted and in force 27 July 2026, as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 per one source.
  - High-risk obligations delayed: Annex III to 2 Dec 2027, Annex I to 2 Aug 2028.
  - Art 50 left on the 2 Aug 2026 schedule.
  - Generative systems already on the market before 2 Aug 2026 have until 2 Dec 2026 to meet Art 50(2) marking.
  - One source says the Commission adopted Art 50 guidelines on 20 July 2026.
  - Sources: [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/); [Jones Walker](https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/ai-law-blog/yes-august-2-still-matters-the-eu-approved-a-high-risk-ai-delay-but-most-trans.html); [Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/eu-ai-act-high-risk-delay-article-50-transparency-consent/) [S].
- **ESMA statement on AI in retail investment services** (ESMA35-335435667-5924, 30 May 2024).
  - MiFID II applies in full when firms use AI, including third-party tools.
  - Management bodies are responsible.
  - Firms must ensure AI-produced information, marketing included, is accurate, be transparent with clients about AI used in interactions, and test, monitor and keep records.
  - Source: [ESMA statement](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2024-05/ESMA35-335435667-5924__Public_Statement_on_AI_and_investment_services.pdf) [K] (medium-high).
- **FCA.** Technology-neutral: existing rules (Principles, Consumer Duty, SM&CR, COBS 4) apply to AI-generated communications. — [FCA AI Update (April 2024)](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/corporate-documents/artificial-intelligence-ai-update-further-response-ai-white-paper) [K] (medium)
- **AI use in UK wealth firms.** About 13% use AI now, and up to 45% use or are considering it within 12 months (FCA 2026 survey). — [FCA wealth management survey report 2026](https://www.fca.org.uk/data/wealth-management-survey-report-2026) [S]
- **Likeness and image rights.**
  - UK: there is no standalone image right, but false endorsement is actionable in passing off (Irvine v Talksport [2002] EWHC 367 (Ch)). — [BAILII](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWHC/Ch/2002/367.html) [K] (high on the case; medium on the URL)
  - Germany: KUG §22 allows a person's image to be disseminated only with their consent. — [KUG §22](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/kunsturhg/__22.html) [K] (high)
  - GDPR Art 9 treats biometric data used for unique identification as special-category data. — [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) [K] (high)
- **France: influencer law labels.** It requires "image virtuelle" on AI-generated images and "image retouchée" on retouched images in influencer content. — [Loi n° 2023-451](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000047663185) [K] (medium-high)
- **Microsoft Advertising** keeps auto-generated ad assets opt-in for financial services. — [Microsoft Jan 2026 product updates](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/january-2026/performance-max-updates-and-other-product-news-for-january-2026) [S]

### Inferences
How common uses map to the rules:
- **AI-assisted copy that compliance reviews and approves:** green. Marketing copy under editorial responsibility needs no Art 50(4) text disclosure.
- **AI images:** marking is the tool provider's job. Disclose only if the image is a deep fake: a realistic depiction of real people, places or events that would falsely appear authentic.
- **Synthetic portfolio-manager avatar or voice:** amber.
  - EU: Art 50(4) disclosure at first exposure.
  - UK: disclose wherever viewers could otherwise believe the PM actually said it (fair, clear and not misleading; CAP).
  - Everywhere: a written licence covering scope, media, duration, revocation and what happens when the PM leaves; approval of each script; no unscripted generation.
  - Publish an official verification channel, because fund-manager impersonation scams use the same technology.
- **Website chatbots for fund buyers:**
  - If built in-house, the manager is the Art 50(1) provider.
  - Answers are promotions or marketing communications, so restrict the bot to approved data and wording, keep logs, and place it behind the professional gate.
- **AI-personalised dynamic creative:** generate only from an approved claims library with automated checks. Any new claim needs human approval. Archive every variant (COBS 4.11; ESMA record expectations).
- **Agent-accessible fund data (APIs or MCP servers feeding AI assistants):**
  - Expose only data already approved and published, with the context needed to keep it compliant: dates, share class, fee basis and standard warnings.
  - Put professional-only data behind authentication.
  - Assume third-party AI may strip warnings, so avoid promotional framing in the data itself.

- **Italy, from prior knowledge (medium) and unverified:** Law 132/2025 (in force 10 Oct 2025) created an offence of unlawfully disseminating AI-generated or altered content that causes unjust harm.

## 12. Influencers and creators: FCA/ASA, France, Italy, Spain, and relevance to B2B "professional creator" sponsorships

### Takeaway
- For B2B "professional creators" (adviser-community LinkedIn voices, fund-selector podcasts and newsletters), the statutory influencer regimes rarely apply because their thresholds are high.
- The core rules do apply:
  - the content is the manager's financial promotion or marketing communication, so it needs approval, balance, disclaimers and records;
  - it must be labelled as advertising;
  - payments can be inducements if the creator works for a firm that advises clients or manages their money.

### Cited Findings
- **UK: FG24/1** expects firms to approve and oversee influencer and affiliate promotions, and treats unapproved promotion by an unauthorised influencer as potentially criminal (s21/s25). The approver-gateway exemption lets a firm approve its own promotions for creators to carry. — [FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/finalised-guidance/fg24-1-finalised-guidance-financial-promotions-social-media); [PS23/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/policy-statements/ps23-13-introducing-gateway-firms-approving-financial-promotions) [K] (medium-high)
- **UK: ASA/CAP.** CAP Code rule 2.1 says marketing communications must be obviously identifiable as such; the CAP/CMA influencer guide sets labelling practice. — [CAP Code section 2](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/02.html); [Influencers' guide](https://www.asa.org.uk/resource/influencers-guide.html) [K] (medium)
- **UK: other law.** The Business Protection from Misleading Marketing Regulations 2008 cover misleading B2B advertising. The DMCC Act 2024's consumer-protection regime (in force 6 April 2025) is consumer-facing. — [BPRs 2008](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2008/1276/contents); [DMCC Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/contents) [K] (high/medium)
- **France: Loi n° 2023-451 (9 June 2023) on commercial influence.**
  - Requires a clear "Publicité" or "Collaboration commerciale" label throughout.
  - Bans influencer promotion of certain products, including risky financial contracts covered by the CMF electronic-advertising ban, and crypto-asset services except from authorised providers.
  - Requires written contracts above a threshold and makes advertiser and influencer jointly liable.
  - Influencers outside the EU who target France must appoint a representative.
  - Amended by Ordonnance 2024-978 (Nov 2024) to bring it into line with EU law.
  - Source: [Loi n° 2023-451](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000047663185) [K] (medium-high on the main features; medium on details).
- **EU level:**
  - DSA Art 26(2) requires platforms to let users declare commercial content;
  - the UCPD bans undisclosed paid editorial content in B2C practices (Annex I point 11);
  - B2B advertising falls under Directive 2006/114 (misleading and comparative advertising).
  - Sources: [DSA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj); [UCPD 2005/29](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2005/29/oj); [Directive 2006/114](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2006/114/oj) [K] (high).
- **Germany.** UWG §5a(4) requires commercial purposes to be disclosed, so paid creator posts need a "Werbung" or "Anzeige" label. — [UWG §5a](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/uwg_2004/__5a.html) [K] (high)
- **LinkedIn Thought Leader Ads** carry a "promoted" tag and run the creator's original post unedited; creators approve each sponsorship request. — [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/thought-leader-ads) [S]

### Inferences
Workable structure for a B2B creator sponsorship:
- **Contract:**
  - scope;
  - compliance pre-approval of every script and post;
  - mandatory local-language labels ("Ad", "Sponsored", "Werbung", "Publicité" / "Collaboration commerciale", "Pubblicità", "Publicidad");
  - takedown and correction rights;
  - records;
  - no performance claims unless the full compliant set is included;
  - restricting product-specific content to jurisdictions where the fund is registered (the creator's audience is usually multi-country).
- **Due diligence** (FG24/1): the creator's past content, competence and audience composition.
- **Inducement screen:** no payments to practising advisers or discretionary managers (section 5).
- **Thought Leader Ads with external creators:** sponsor only pre-approved posts, because they cannot be edited.
- **France:** a paid LinkedIn creator promoting a fund to the public online may count as an "influenceur" regardless of audience size, which brings in the labelling and written-contract duties. Confirm with French counsel.

### Gaps
- **Italy, AGCOM Delibera 7/24/CONS (Jan 2024).** From prior knowledge, unverified: it covers "relevant influencers" (about 1m+ followers on a platform, with activity and engagement thresholds) under the audiovisual code, including disclosure and protection of minors. Codes of conduct and a register reportedly followed in 2025, with lower thresholds.
- **Spain, Royal Decree 444/2024 (30 April 2024) on "users of special relevance".** From prior knowledge, unverified: thresholds of €300k+ annual income from the activity, plus 1m followers on one platform or 2m in aggregate, plus 24+ videos a year. These users must register and follow audiovisual advertising rules. The CNMV's treatment of finfluencers under Circular 2/2020 is also unverified.
- Not verified: AMF and BaFin positions on paid finfluencers, and when soliciting through creators becomes "démarchage" in France.
- Not verified: the EU Retail Investment Strategy's final MiFID finfluencer provisions (proposal: written agreements, records, the firm's responsibility for marketing by third parties it pays); and whether the Commission has published its Digital Fairness Act proposal (influencer marketing, dark patterns).

## 13. When "professional" fund-buyer audiences are legally retail, and what follows

### Takeaway
Most institutional gatekeepers are per se professional. Several target segments may legally be retail or uncertain:
- individuals acting outside their professional capacity;
- sole-trader or partnership IFAs (also PECR "individual subscribers");
- many single family offices;
- German §34f trade-licensed intermediaries;
- unlicensed Swiss advisers;
- individuals working within Italian adviser networks.

Getting this wrong turns professional-only marketing (national private placement, unrecognised funds, Swiss non-approved funds) into unlawful retail marketing.

### Cited Findings
- **UK, COBS 3.5.2R.** Per se professional clients include entities required to be authorised or regulated to operate in the financial markets, large undertakings and institutional investors.
  - Elective professional status (COBS 3.5.3R) needs a qualitative assessment and, for MiFID business, two of three quantitative tests:
    - at least 10 significant transactions a quarter over the previous four quarters;
    - a portfolio over €500k;
    - at least one year working in the financial sector.
  - Source: [COBS 3.5](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/3/5.html) [K] (high).
- **EU, MiFID II Annex II.** Per se professionals include authorised or regulated financial institutions, collective investment schemes and their management companies, pension funds, insurers, and large undertakings meeting two of: balance sheet €20m, net turnover €40m, own funds €2m. It also sets the opt-up criteria. — [MiFID II](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2014/65/oj) [K] (high)
- **UK, FPO Art 19(5)** covers employees and officers of authorised and exempt firms when they receive a communication in that capacity (see section 1). — [FPO art 19](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1529/article/19) [K] (high)
- **Switzerland, FinSA Arts 4–5.** FINMA-licensed portfolio managers are per se professional as financial intermediaries. — [FinSA](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2019/758/en) [K] (high); the licensed population is 1,664 ([FINMA report 2025](https://report.finma.ch/2025/en/market-developments/market-developments-in-the-asset-management-industry) [S]).
- **Segment sizes that matter for status:**
  - UK: 5,548 advice firms and about 31,000 advisers ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/news-stories/fca-publishes-findings-financial-adviser-market-survey) [S]);
  - Germany: 41,388 §34f intermediaries and about 375 §34h fee-based advisers at 1 July 2026 ([DIHK](https://www.dihk.de/de/serviceportal/fuer-gewerbetreibende-unternehmer/statistiken-und-bestandszahlen-zum-vermittlerregister-141724) [S]);
  - Spain: 88 EAFs and 52 EAFNs at end-2024 ([CNMV](https://www.cnmv.es/webservices/verdocumento/ver?e=ZBKbShY+9GuHqrBgZVA71KEpI+iFfPy6xtr5zzoQYHmkKR5xrcqgTLzGLccEARss) [S]);
  - France: CIF population and association structure ([AMF](https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/private/2025-12/chiffres-cles-cif-2024.pdf) [S]).
- **UK sole traders.** A secondary write-up of FCA register data reports that about 10% of UK adviser firms are sole traders and 77% private limited companies. The figures in that source are internally inconsistent, so treat them as indicative only. — [FCA, understanding the financial advice market](https://www.fca.org.uk/data/understanding-financial-advice-market) [S] (low)

### Inferences
Likely status by segment:
- **UK:**
  - authorised IFA firms, their employees (in capacity), appointed representatives, DFMs, platforms, multi-managers, private banks and insurers: professional / investment professionals;
  - paraplanners: usually in scope where their role involves investment activity; borderline for administrative staff;
  - sole traders: authorised, so investment professionals for FPO purposes, but individual subscribers for PECR email and plainly personal data;
  - single family offices: often unregulated and not "large", so not per se professional. Use FPO high-net-worth or sophisticated-investor routes, or treat them as retail.
- **EU:**
  - banks, private banks, insurers (unit-linked teams), management companies and Spanish EAFs: per se professional;
  - French CIFs, Italian independent advisers registered with OCF, Spanish EAFNs: regulated, but per se status depends on local transposition (specialist advice);
  - Italian *consulenti finanziari* (bank or network agents): individuals whose principal is the client;
  - German §34f/§34h intermediaries: trade-licensed and often sole traders. Their per se status is uncertain, so treat them as retail for UK OEICs marketed under national private placement unless German counsel confirms otherwise.
- **Switzerland:**
  - FINMA-licensed independent asset managers: professional;
  - unlicensed advisers or "family offices" without professional treasury: private clients unless they opt out. Offering non-approved foreign funds to them risks being an offer to non-qualified investors.
- **Operational consequences:**
  - suppression lists by segment for any professional-only fund;
  - a stricter email regime for UK sole traders and partnerships;
  - ABM lists built from regulator registers, with an entity-type field.

### Gaps
- Not verified: how French CIFs, Italian independent advisers, Spanish EAFNs and German §34f/§34h intermediaries are categorised under each country's MiFID transposition. Needs specialist advice.
- No authoritative UK breakdown of sole-trader versus partnership advice firms was found.

## 14. Synthesis: traffic-light assessment by tactic and jurisdiction, and the controls that make each workable

### Takeaway
**Green, the strongest precision routes:**
- account-based targeting on the platforms' own professional data;
- gated, compliance-approved creative;
- campaigns run by or for the entity entitled to market in each country.

**Amber, workable with controls:**
- CRM matching;
- intent data;
- visitor identification;
- dynamic live-data creative;
- creators;
- AI content;
- distributor sponsorships.

**Red:**
- precise geofencing in strict EU markets;
- person-level de-anonymisation;
- cold email in Germany, Spain and Switzerland without consent;
- paying practising advisers or buying fund-list prominence;
- targeting countries where the fund or share class is not registered.

**Controls that turn amber into green:**
- **Registration and entity matrix.** Map fund × share class × country × investor type × advertising entity. Feed it into platform geo-targeting and landing-page gates. Re-check it monthly and on every de-notification (UCITS Art 93a).
- **Professional-audience evidence pack.** Keep:
  - register-sourced account lists;
  - function and seniority layers with exclusions;
  - audience expansion and off-platform networks switched off;
  - professional-only and capital-at-risk statements inside the creative;
  - the gate log;
  - delivery and demographic reports;
  - a leakage-test record.
- **Creative compliance system.** Pre-approve templates for dynamic or AI variants; use a claims library; run automated checks for disclaimers and the past-performance set; archive every rendered variant with its approval (COBS 4.11; ManCo records); add an EU ESMA disclaimer overlay; route EU assets through the ManCo.
- **Consent and data architecture.** Run a CMP with Google Consent Mode v2 and IAB TCF where relevant. Separate the "advertising" and "B2B company identification" consent purposes in the EU. Use server-side IP handling with no person-level resolution. Publish a privacy notice naming ad-platform matching and enrichment vendors. Keep a DPIA and LIA per tactic. Rely on DPF or UK data bridge certification for US vendors. Suppress objections across channels.
- **Country rules for email.** Germany, Spain and Switzerland (and probably Italy) need consent or soft-opt-in-only lists. UK and France can rely on legitimate interests for corporate or professional addresses, with an opt-out. UK sole traders and partnerships need consent.
- **Creator and sponsorship governance.** Use contracts with approval rights and local disclosure labels. Run an inducement screen (no practising advisers, no fund-list placement), arm's-length rate cards not linked to flows, and minor-benefit logs for CPD and hospitality, with FinSA Art 26 disclosure in Switzerland.
- **AI governance.** Assign Art 50 roles (provider or deployer). Disclose deep fakes. Hold portfolio-manager likeness licences. Keep humans in the loop and keep logs, in line with ESMA's May 2024 statement. Run AI-literacy training (AI Act Art 4).
- **Platform readiness.** Complete Google, Microsoft and (where applicable) Meta financial-services verification for each advertising entity before launch. Check OpenAI approval before planning ChatGPT placements. Expect EU limits on LinkedIn messaging and targeting.
