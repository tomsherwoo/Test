# Measuring and proving the impact of B2B digital marketing on UK and European professional fund buyers: attribution, benchmarks, case studies and operating model (2022–2026)

Research note scope: wholesale/intermediary fund buyers (UK advisers/paraplanners, DFMs/MPS teams, multi-managers, private banks, platform fund-list teams, European bank/private-bank selectors, etc.). Assumed advertiser: a boutique-to-mid-sized UK-headquartered active manager distributing UK OEICs and Irish/Luxembourg UCITS. Labels used: [Vendor] = vendor-reported, no independent support; [Survey] = industry survey; [Academic]; [US] or [Global] = outside UK/Europe but transferable; [Unverified] = based only on a search-engine summary that could not be checked against the page (see the research-constraint note in Section 1 Gaps).

## 1. Attribution to flows: how can digital campaign exposure be linked to account-level fund-buyer engagement and fund flows, and what data and methods make this possible?

### Takeaway
No source found shows a UK or European asset manager publishing a validated, account-level "campaign → fund flows" attribution. The US has advisor-level transaction and intent data (Broadridge Distribution Insight plus AdvisorTarget) that makes such attribution possible. The UK and Europe now have comparable building blocks, mostly consolidated through 2024–2025 deals and launches: platform flow intelligence (FE fundinfo's acquisition of Finscape into Nexus; Fundscape), FCA-register account universes (ISS MI's purchase of Autus), distributor browsing and flow data (Allfunds) and fund-buyer brand surveys (Broadridge Fund Buyer Focus). The most defensible design is incrementality, meaning randomised account holdouts plus leading indicators plus long-lag flow reads, rather than multi-touch attribution. Academic evidence supports this: advertising effects on flows are conditional on performance, convex in spend, and work mainly by stemming redemptions.

**Academic evidence on advertising and fund flows [Academic; mostly US/Nordic retail; useful for model design]**
- Gallaher, Kaniel & Starks, "Advertising and Mutual Funds: From Families to Individual Funds" (CEPR DP10329):
  - Industry-level advertising raises flows, even to fund families that do not advertise.
  - At family level the effect is convex: only "high relative advertisers" see a significant positive effect.
  - At individual-fund level, advertising mainly stems redemptions rather than increasing purchases.
  - Advertising dampens flow-performance sensitivity for poor performers and increases it for strong ones. — [CEPR](https://cepr.org/publications/DP10329); [Causal Claims summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/DP10329.html)
- A study of about 471,000 fund-company advertisements (1997–2003) found advertising generally ineffective at attracting inflows, though more effective during the bear market. [Unverified] The search summary attributes this to a 2011 *Financial Management* paper, "The Effects of Advertising on Mutual Fund Flows: Results from a New Database". — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1755-053X.2011.01161.x)
- Finnish evidence: neither past performance nor advertising alone is enough to increase cash flows, but the two together significantly affect them. [Unverified attribution] — [Aalto working paper (likely source)](https://beta.aalto.fi/sites/g/files/flghsv161/files/2018-12/advertising_and_fund_flows.pdf)
- Family spillover: advertised funds can significantly raise flows to other, higher-performing funds in the same family. — [SSRN: Wei, Chen & Peng](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1779584)
- Marketing-mix modelling mechanics: adstock and lag (carryover) effects, plus diminishing returns modelled with response curves. — [Ekimetrics](https://www.ekimetrics.com/articles/marketing-mix-modeling-how-to-measure-marketing-impact-and-optimize-budget-allocation)
- Recent methods research calibrates MMM parameters from geo-experiments. [Only the title was seen] — [arXiv 2608.21128](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21128)

**Long-lag and brand-measurement frameworks**
- The 95:5 rule (John Dawes, Ehrenberg-Bass, published through LinkedIn's B2B Institute in 2021): up to 95% of business buyers are out of market at any one time. For example, corporations change their principal bank or law firm about once every five years, which implies roughly 5% are in market per quarter. Dawes says the figure is not meant to be precise and depends on the category's purchase cycle. — [Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/peter-weinberg-jon-lombardo-95-5-rule); [CustomerThink](https://customerthink.com/what-the-955-rule-means-for-b2b-marketing/)
- Share of search (Les Binet, IPA EffWorks Global 2020): a brand's share of organic searches in its category, from free Google Trends data going back to 2004. It predicted market share with a lead of 9–12 months for cars, about 6 months for handsets and 0–3 months for energy. It is not a perfect predictor, because price moves and negative searches add noise. — [IPA](https://ipa.co.uk/news/binet-presents-fast-cheap-predictive-share-of-search-metric/); [WARC](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/news/share-of-search-can-predict-market-share/44232); [Campaign](https://www.campaignlive.co.uk/article/les-binet-urges-marketers-measure-google-searches-predict-brand-health/1697288)

### Inferences
- **The UK gap is closing.** A US manager can join advisor-level sales data to ad exposure. UK boutiques have mostly relied on aggregate platform market-share data (e.g., Fundscape), plus Finscape's multi-platform flow data since 2020. With the 2024–2025 consolidation into FE fundinfo Nexus and ISS MI MarketPro, the pieces exist to approximate the US approach:
  - an FCA-register account universe (Autus/ISS MI MarketPro: 70k firms, 220k individuals);
  - platform flow intelligence that claims to show "who is making the buying decision" (Finscape in FE fundinfo Nexus);
  - professional browsing data (Allfunds Connect, 800+ institutions).
  - Whether these can be joined at adviser-firm or DFM level for one manager's own funds is the critical unknown to raise in vendor due diligence.
- **Recommended measurement design for a boutique, in place of multi-touch attribution (my synthesis):**
  1. Define 300–1,500 target accounts (adviser firms, DFM/MPS teams, wealth managers, and European selector institutions from Broadridge or Allfunds lists), tiered by flow potential using platform-flow data.
  2. Randomly hold out 20–30% of accounts within each tier from paid media (LinkedIn company-list exclusion), keeping sales coverage constant or logging it as a covariate.
  3. Measure leading indicators monthly: engaged minutes in webinars and LiveSend/Turtl, meeting acceptance, fund-page visits on Allfunds Connect, branded fund-name/ISIN search.
  4. Measure mid-funnel milestones: due-diligence requests and buy-list or MPS inclusion, logged as CRM opportunities.
  5. Read lagging net flows per account over 6–18 months, plus redemption rates among existing holders.
  6. Analyse as a difference-in-differences between exposed and holdout accounts.
- **Why each piece matters:**
  - Holdouts handle the long lags: the 95:5 logic implies only a small share of selectors are reviewing a sector at any time.
  - Tracking redemptions follows Gallaher et al.: advertising mostly stems redemptions, so a measure built only on gross inflows will undercount the effect.
  - The analysis must control for fund performance, because the literature shows flows respond to advertising mainly together with performance.
- **Statistical power is the real constraint at boutique scale.** UK intermediary flows are concentrated in large DFMs and consolidators, so a flow-based test across a few hundred accounts will usually be underpowered. Use leading indicators for in-flight decisions, treat flow reads as confirmation built up across repeated cycles, and stratify on account size.
- **Geo or matched-market tests suit UK fund buyers poorly.** The intermediary market is national, the key accounts are few, and LinkedIn's regional granularity is coarse. A test across countries (e.g., Italy vs Spain advisers for a cross-border UCITS) is more realistic for continental Europe.
- **B2B MMM needs a long, varied spend history and many periods.** That is generally infeasible for a single boutique except at fund-range level using Fundscape or IA-sector share as the dependent variable (inference).
- **Creative measurement ideas grounded in the sources:**
  - A "professional share of search": the fund's share of Allfunds Connect visits and comparisons within its peer group, by region and entity type, read alongside Google Trends for fund names (Binet's method).
  - A "switch-capture" metric from Nexus/Finscape fund-switch analytics: share of switches out of rival funds in the same sector going to your fund, exposed accounts vs holdout.
  - A brand-consideration panel read from Broadridge Fund Buyer Focus, which interviews more than 1,000 fund buyers a year.
- **Multi-touch attribution and "influenced pipeline"** are best kept as diagnostics. Fund-selection decisions are made by committees, partly offline (meetings, due-diligence questionnaires) and over long periods, so touch-level credit is unreliable (inference, not tested in the sources).

## 2. Benchmarks: what quantitative benchmarks exist for costs, engagement and conversion rates, budgets and channel mix?

### Takeaway
Credible public benchmarks for reaching European professional fund buyers are thin. LinkedIn cost benchmarks come almost entirely from agency blogs with undisclosed methodology. For narrowly targeted financial audiences they cluster around CPMs of $50–$100, CTRs of 0.3–0.9% and CPCs of $8–$15, with "finance & insurance" averages pulled down by consumer audiences. ON24 reports financial-services webinars average 185 attendees and 46 minutes of engagement. Cerulli, FCS and Alpha FMC surveys show European managers shifting budget and headcount to digital, video, webinars and podcasts, often on flat budgets. No reliable 2022–2026 figure was found for European asset-manager marketing budgets or digital share.

### Inferences
- **Planning arithmetic (illustrative; uses the vendor ranges above):**
  - At a $50–$100 CPM, giving a 15,000-person fund-buyer audience about one impression a week (≈780k impressions a year) costs roughly $39k–$78k a year in LinkedIn media. Within a boutique budget, but frequency, not reach, will be the cost driver.
  - Italy alone has more than 30,000 active advisers (Neuberger case).
  - A 0.3–0.7% CTR at $50–$100 CPM implies roughly $7–$33 per click, consistent with the $8–$15 CPC central range.
- **Benchmark contamination is the main trap.** "Finance & insurance" averages (CPC around $3) mix in consumer audiences. A cheap CPC or high CTR against a professional fund-buyer target is more likely a sign of audience leakage than of outperformance.
- **Treat ON24's 185 attendees and 46 minutes as a ceiling, not a target.** The figures cover banks, insurers and wealth firms. Boutique fund-buyer webinars will usually be smaller, so engaged minutes per target account and meetings booked per webinar are better KPIs than headcount.
- **Channel norms have shifted toward owned audio-visual formats:** 83% host AV content, 76% promote webinars, 40% podcast. Social-media teams of more than four people were becoming common among larger European managers by around 2022. A boutique cannot match that headcount and will need agency or freelance capacity plus repurposing workflows (inference).

### Gaps
- No 2022–2026 benchmark was found for European asset-manager marketing budgets (as a share of revenue or AUM) or for the digital share of spend. The Cerulli figures are 2016 and around 2021; Kurtosys is 2018.
- Not found:
  - email benchmarks specific to asset managers' adviser or fund-buyer lists (opens, clicks);
  - podcast or DOOH effectiveness evidence for B2B financial audiences;
  - CPMs for senior finance audiences outside LinkedIn (programmatic ABM, FT or Bloomberg sponsorship, Dianomi-type native);
  - LinkedIn lead-gen form conversion rates or official LinkedIn financial-services benchmarks;
  - UK- or Europe-specific LinkedIn cost data (all the ranges above are in USD and largely US-derived).
- Higher cold-audience CPL figures ($250–$600+) appeared in a search summary but could not be tied to a specific source, so they are excluded.

## 3. Case studies: which documented creative digital campaigns aimed at professional fund buyers, without reliance on trade publications, exist, with what objectives, tactics and results?

### Inferences
- **Recurring patterns in the non-trade cases:**
  - Value-exchange education with professional accreditation: Candriam's CPD/CE credits produced about 16k members, an audience the manager owns.
  - Short-form video on LinkedIn with engagement-based retargeting, which builds a pool of engaged advisers rather than optimising for clicks (Neuberger).
  - Measurable formats that replace PDFs, such as interactive documents and tracked sends (AllianzGI/Turtl, LGIM/Seismic). These provide the account-level engagement data needed for the Section 1 measurement design.
  - Interactive email and outlook content recognised by awards (Janus Henderson).
- **None of the documented cases reports flows, assets or buy-list wins.** That is consistent with Section 1: the industry largely reports engagement and efficiency (cost per lead, hours saved), not incrementality. A boutique that published a holdout-based result would stand out.
- **The most transferable ideas for a UK boutique (my synthesis):**
  - A CPD-accredited micro-academy on the boutique's specialism (e.g., a niche asset class) for UK advisers, paraplanners and DFM analysts, plus EFPA-accredited versions for continental selectors, with registrants matched to the CRM account universe.
  - Fund-manager-led LinkedIn video sequences retargeted on view-through thresholds.
  - Tracked interactive factsheets and outlooks in place of PDFs.
- **Vendor ABM case libraries lack asset-manager stories.** This suggests (unconfirmed) that IP-based ABM platforms are little used, or rarely publicised, for European wholesale fund buyers. That fits a fragmented audience working from home and the GDPR constraints on IP-level identification.

## 4. Operating model: what team structures, martech and compliance stack and workflows sit behind high-performing programmes, and what are the common failure modes?

### Takeaway
The evidence points to a maturity gap. Large managers run sales-enablement platforms with engagement tracking (Seismic is reportedly used by most of the top 10 global managers), interactive content tools, outsourced content operations and growing social and video teams. Surveys meanwhile show most firms historically rated their digital maturity basic or developing, and many digitise on flat budgets. AI compliance pre-screening (Saifr, launched by Fidelity in 2022) exists but is built around US FINRA and SEC rules. The common failure modes that can be documented are vanity metrics, cheap but wrong audiences, under-concentrated spend and measuring only gross inflows.

### Cited Findings

**Sales enablement and content operations**
- Seismic positions itself as "the leading content automation solution for wholesalers and marketing teams" and shows which documents are used in client interactions. [Vendor] — [Seismic asset management](https://seismic.com/asset-management)

### Inferences
- **Lean operating model for a boutique (my synthesis):**
  - One marketing-operations and data lead who owns the account universe (FCA register plus European distributor lists), CRM hygiene, LinkedIn and ABM audiences, holdout design and reporting.
  - One or two content leads with protected time from fund managers.
  - A named compliance partner working "in the loop", with a pre-approved claims and disclosure library, modular templates (fund-manager video, chart cards, outlook snippets) and a fast lane for low-risk recycled content.
  - An agency or freelancers for video, design and paid media.
- **Stack implied by the cases:**
  - CRM (Salesforce or Dynamics) with opportunities defined as fund-buyer milestones (due diligence started, buy-list or MPS inclusion).
  - A marketing automation platform.
  - Tracked content delivery: Seismic at the top end, or lighter tools such as Turtl.
  - External data feeds: FE fundinfo Nexus/Finscape, Fundscape, Allfunds and Broadridge Fund Buyer Focus.
- **Compliance speed is the binding constraint on high-volume digital creative.** US-built AI pre-screeners (Saifr) would need configuring for the UK and EU (FCA financial-promotion rules; ESMA marketing-communication guidelines for UCITS). That is an assumption to validate, not a finding.
- **Likely failure modes, ranked by how strongly the evidence supports them:**
  1. Optimising to CTR or CPL and so drifting toward retail or consumer-finance audiences (strong).
  2. Reporting hits and engagement as success with no link to accounts or flows (strong).
  3. Spreading a small budget across many funds and markets, below the "high relative advertiser" threshold where effects appear (moderate, by analogy with the academic evidence).
  4. Judging campaigns only on gross inflows within a quarter, ignoring redemption retention and the 6–18-month selection cycle (moderate).
  5. Over-gating content, poor CRM data and low LinkedIn match rates. These are plausible but not evidenced in the sources found.

### Gaps
- Not covered by any source found:
  - benchmarks for approval cycle times or speed to market in asset-manager marketing;
  - case studies of Red Marker, Smarsh or other AI compliance tools at UK or European asset managers;
  - how ESMA's marketing-communications guidelines and FCA financial-promotion rules apply to LinkedIn targeting of professional investors (the FCA site was blocked by the egress policy during this session);
  - surveys of martech prevalence among European asset managers (Salesforce Marketing Cloud or Account Engagement, HubSpot, Dynamics 365, Kurtosys, Sitecore, Adobe, Contentful, Bynder);
  - team-size benchmarks for boutique or mid-sized managers.
- No Kurtosys survey from 2022–2026 was found; the latest surfaced was the 2018 edition.
