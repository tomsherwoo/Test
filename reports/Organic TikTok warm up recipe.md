# Warm Up TikTok by Copying Real Users

A natural warm-up is not a ritual but simply using a new account the way measured real users do, and those numbers are modest. In practice that means **about 20–30 minutes a day across three or four short opens**, roughly 60–100 videos a day with a median of 6–13 seconds before each swipe and **about 45% watched to the end**, and a like on roughly **one video in twenty** at first (established users like one in eleven). Add **two to four follows a day**, each after a look at the creator's profile, plus one to three searches, almost no comments or shares for the first few days, and a **first original post around day four**, building from every other day to roughly daily by the third week. TikTok publishes no warm-up rule and no numeric activity caps; its Community Guidelines ban automation, bulk or repetitive actions, bought engagement and like-for-like solicitation, and its systems strip fake activity at industrial scale, with **86.3 million fake accounts removed in Q1 2026** and over 36 billion fake likes blocked in the first half of 2024. The often-quoted limits of about 200 follows, 500 likes and 200 comments a day are unconfirmed user and vendor reports, and a human-pace recipe uses roughly 1–10% of even the most conservative of them, so the real risks for one person on their own phone are bursts, copy-pasted comments, reposted content and over-posting, not a skipped waiting period. Independent audits show the For You feed learns a niche within a few hundred videos, which three days of ordinary scrolling already supplies, and nothing official or measured shows that a creator's own viewing history changes how far their videos travel. Nearly every detailed warm-up schedule comes from vendors selling automation, accounts, VPNs or scheduling tools; none cites data, and their precise prescriptions (7–14-day waits, blank profiles, watching everything to the end, "200-view jail") are unverified folklore.

## TikTok polices bots and bursts, not skipped warm-ups

TikTok's rules never mention warming up an account. The current Community Guidelines (the 2026H2 update, effective 24 September 2026) describe authentic engagement as what "helps power the recommendations you see". They state "We strictly prohibit automation tools, scripts, or other tricks designed to bypass our systems", and they ban "the trade of services that artificially boost engagement or trick the recommendation system" ([TikTok Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/safety/en/policies-and-engagement/integrity-authenticity); [Open Terms Archive capture](https://github.com/OpenTermsArchive/pga-versions/blob/main/TikTok/Community%20Guidelines.md)). The 2025H2 version, whose main text is word-for-word identical, has more detailed lists. They define spam as "Using automation to run many accounts or send repetitive content", "Posting a large amount of irrelevant material" and "Using bots or scripts to write fake reviews or comments, or to increase likes or shares". They also make "'Like-for-like' promises" and "False incentives for gifting or following" ineligible for the For You feed (FYF) ([Open Terms Archive, 2025H2](https://github.com/OpenTermsArchive/contrib-versions/blob/main/TikTok/Community%20Guidelines.md)). Penalties start with removing "fake likes, followers, or other inflated signals". Next comes limiting an account's ability "to post new content, appear in top search results, or in the FYF", and the most severe is banning the account and any new ones its owner creates. The UK/EEA Terms (July 2026) also ban "inauthentic commercial behaviours such as operating spam or impersonation accounts" and unapproved automated data extraction ([TikTok Terms via Open Terms Archive](https://github.com/OpenTermsArchive/pga-versions/blob/main/TikTok/Terms%20of%20Service.md)). **Every prohibited category describes automation, bulk repetition, purchased engagement or solicitation. Nothing in the official text caps or penalises varied manual use.** TikTok's getting-started material covers a personalised username, photo and bio, original content, and a posting target you can consistently hit. It sets no waiting period before the first post ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/articles/get-started/); [Creator Academy on cadence](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/posting-cadence-best-practices-publishers)). Even MonetizedNow, which sells accounts and publishes a 7–14-day programme, concedes that "TikTok has never published a warm-up rule" ([MonetizedNow](https://monetizednow.com/warm-up-tiktok-account)).

What TikTok does publish is the scale of its automated enforcement, and that scale is why pace matters more than calendars. It removed **86,288,705 fake accounts in Q1 2026**, with a 99.3% proactive removal rate ([ARY News](https://arynews.tv/tiktok-releases-q1-2026-community-guidelines-enforcement-report)). In Q2 2026, automated systems made 97.4% of video removals, and TikTok says better detection let it remove more fake engagement from comment sections ([TikTok enforcement report](https://www.tiktok.com/safety/en/transparency/community-guidelines-enforcement)). In the first half of 2024 it **prevented over 36 billion fake likes and 15 billion fake follow requests** before they registered. Only 379 million likes and 207 million followers were removed after the fact ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-eu/how-tiktok-counters-deceptive-behaviour)). It also reports "spam accounts blocked at creation" as a separate metric: more than 400,000 during Romania's 2024 election period alone ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/continuing-to-protect-the-integrity-of-tiktok-during-romanian-elections?lang=en-150)). Prevented actions outnumber removed ones by roughly 70–100 to 1, so most enforcement is silent: bursty taps from a new account are more likely to quietly not count than to trigger a warning. When TikTok does react visibly to "suspicious activity", its stated first step is a verification challenge: SMS or email confirmation, a CAPTCHA, or a login from a verified device ([TikTok Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines)). Formal strikes are counted per feature, such as comments or DMs, and expire after 90 days ([TikTok Help](https://www.tiktok.com/en/safety-hc/account-and-user-safety/content-violations-and-bans)).

No TikTok page states a daily or hourly cap on follows, likes, comments or DMs; searches of TikTok's own domain turn up only user videos and Discover pages. The figures in circulation come from users, SEO pages and growth-tool vendors, and they disagree with one another. One outreach-tool vendor even argues that a fixed daily follow limit "doesn't exist" ([hooka.to](https://hooka.to/blog/tiktok-follow-limits/)). Treat them as ceilings to stay far beneath. Reported blocks escalate in a consistent pattern even though TikTok publishes no durations for ordinary users. A first "You're tapping too fast" message reportedly lasts 15 minutes to an hour, and 20–30 minutes is the most common report. Brand-new or repeat-offending accounts reportedly wait one to three days, and a persistent pattern can mean one or two weeks. "Following too frequently" blocks reportedly last 24–48 hours, and retrying during a block reportedly extends it ([Ricky Spears](https://www.rickyspears.com/tiktok/tiktok-tapping-too-fast/); [Nairobi News](https://nairobinews.co.ke/how-to-take-off-youre-tapping-too-fast-on-tiktok/); [TikTok Discover](https://www.tiktok.com/discover/if-you-have-followed-too-many-people-on-tiktok-how-long-does-it-take-to-let-you-follow-again?lang=en)). The 10,000-account following cap is the most consistent report of all, but TikTok has not confirmed it. Nor does a warm-up unlock any features. An adult account can post, like, follow and comment from its first minute, and DMs need age 16+. LIVE, gifting and monetisation need age 18+ plus thresholds that only followers and views can meet. Going LIVE has a follower threshold that varies by market, commonly reported as 1,000, and Creator Rewards needs 10,000 followers and 100,000 views in 30 days ([TikTok Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/safety/en/policies-and-engagement/accounts-features); [TikTok Help](https://www.tiktok.com/support/faq_detail?id=7581821550694013452&category=web_account)).

## Real users scroll about 27 minutes, finish 45% of videos and like one in eleven

The best evidence on ordinary viewing comes from data donations, in which users hand researchers their own TikTok histories. Zannettou and colleagues' CHI 2024 study covered 347 participants and 9.2 million video views and found a **median of 1,622 seconds (about 27 minutes) a day**, with the lightest quarter under 14 minutes and the heaviest quarter above 48 ([Zannettou et al.](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~franzi/pdf/zannettou-tiktok-chi24.pdf)). Ofcom's passive-metering panel, working independently, put the **average UK adult user at 28 minutes a day** in May 2025, and 18–34s at 49 minutes ([Ofcom Online Nation 2025](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/online-research/online-nation/2025/online-nations-report-2025.pdf?v=409837)). App-panel figures of 70–97 minutes are means dragged up by heavy users. New users start lower and climb. In the Washington Post's donation project of about 1,100 people, light users started near **30 minutes a day**; more than three-quarters were watching nearly twice as long a month later, and the group averaged about 70 minutes within a few months ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/wellness/interactive/2025/tiktok-addiction-algorithm-scrolling-mental-health/)). Zannettou's participants roughly doubled their daily time after 80 days, and among about 7,000 new adopters in a US Android panel, only the top tenth exceeded 30 minutes a day ([Agarwal & Ananthakrishnan](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2024.08260); [UW Foster School](https://foster.uw.edu/news/research-uttara-ananthakrishnan-tiktok-addiction/)). **A new adult account spending 15–30 minutes a day is therefore typical, while two hours from day one looks like a heavy or teenage user.** No study isolates the first week after account creation, so the recipe's earliest days are extrapolated from these light-user cohorts.

That time arrives in short bursts. The Post's light users opened the app **about four times a day**, rising to about eight over five months, while heavy users opened it about 20 times. Across groups that works out to a fairly stable **8–12 minutes per open**. The Wrapped "State of the Feed" panel holds 16,981 donations covering 464 million views; it is self-selected and skewed toward heavy users. Counting views less than 30 minutes apart as one session, it records 3.9–4.7 sessions per active day, with median session lengths of 19–26 minutes ([Wrapped](https://wrapped.vantezzen.io/state-of-the-feed)). Use leans toward the evening. 11% of Wrapped's viewing fell between midnight and 6 am, a late-night tail that grows with habit, and engagement on posts peaks between 6 and 11 pm ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/best-time-to-post-on-tiktok/)). Pew finds that 37% of US adults use TikTok but 24% use it daily. If both figures are shares of all adults, roughly a third of adult users skip days ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/03/02/8-facts-about-americans-and-tiktok/)). An occasional light or missed day is ordinary.

Most videos are passed over quickly. Zannettou's median user saw **about 90 videos a day** (interquartile range 41–170), implying a mean of roughly 18 seconds per video, but swipe times are right-skewed: Wrapped measured a **median of 6–10 seconds** before a swipe, and the Post measured 11–13. Zannettou's attention metric shows the shape of a real scroll: **45% of views were watched to the end, 24% were abandoned before 20% of the clip had played, and 40% before the halfway point**. Most participants finished 30–50% of their videos, and that share stayed flat as their usage grew ([Zannettou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04945)). On a 15–30-second clip, abandoning within the first fifth means leaving within 3–6 seconds. Nobody publishes organic rewatch or loop rates, and blog claims such as "71% of viewers decide within 3 seconds" cite no primary source.

Explicit engagement is rarer than warm-up guides imply. Wrapped's panelists **liked 88 of every 1,000 videos they watched (8.8%)**, and "91% of videos are watched without so much as a like" ([Wrapped](https://wrapped.vantezzen.io/state-of-the-feed)). Liking grows with tenure. Zannettou found it rose about 2× after 120 days for videos from followed creators and 1.5× for others ([Zannettou et al., ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642433)). So a new account's like rate is plausibly **around 4–6%, or one video in 17–25**, though that is an extrapolation, not a measurement. Follows build up early. Participants averaged about 40 followed accounts at the start of their observed histories and about 350 after 120 days, a mean of about 2.6 a day that heavy followers inflate. Pew's observation of real US accounts puts the **median established user at 144–154 follows**: 63 for people who never post and 246 for people who do ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/02/22/how-u-s-adults-use-tiktok/); [Pew Data Labs](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2024/10/08/who-u-s-adults-follow-on-tiktok/)). No donation study reports comment, share or save rates from the viewer's side, so the only anchors are creator-side. Socialinsider's 2025 per-post averages are 3,492 likes, 50 comments and 248 shares. That makes **comments about 1.4% of likes and shares about 7%**; for accounts with 1,000–5,000 followers, shares are closer to 12%. Brand content typically earns about 4% engagement by views. At that rate, the ratios come to roughly half a comment and 2–3 shares per 1,000 views ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/tiktok); [Dash Social](https://www.dashsocial.com/social-media-benchmarks/tiktok)). No major benchmark publishes save rates. About half of US adult users have never posted a video ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/02/22/how-u-s-adults-use-tiktok/)), so a consumption-only first phase is normal.

Search is common but occasional. TikTok-attributed figures say **57% of users use search and 23% search within 30 seconds of opening the app** ([Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/za/blog/tiktok-search); [Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/media/tiktok-ai-powered-tools-woo-marketers-potential-ban/)). Adobe's 2026 survey found that 49% of Americans have used TikTok as a search engine, though only 4% of Gen Z prefer it to Google ([ALM Corp summary of Adobe](https://almcorp.com/blog/tiktok-as-search-engine-2026-data/)). No source reports searches per day. The For You feed dominates everything else. A median of **about 10% of views came from followed accounts**, and that share stayed flat even as follow counts rose from ~40 to ~350 ([Zannettou et al.](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~franzi/pdf/zannettou-tiktok-chi24.pdf)). The Post found users saw creators they don't follow about 90% of the time ([YPulse summary](https://www.ypulse.com/newsfeed/2025/10/14/once-tiktoks-algorithm-learns-users-vibe-its-hard-for-them-to-log-off/)). Nobody has published any measurement of Explore/Discover visits, LIVE viewing time or inbox use. Some users see a "Discover" tab where the Friends tab would be ([TikTok Support](https://support.tiktok.com/en/using-tiktok/exploring-videos/friends-tab?lang=en)). Only 30% of US TikTok users have ever bought through TikTok Shop ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/52193-are-us-tiktok-users-turning-to-tiktok-shop)), so it is normal to ignore these surfaces in the first weeks.

## The feed learns a niche within a few hundred videos

At signup, TikTok invites new users to pick interest categories "like pets or travel". Anyone who skips the step gets "a generalized feed of popular videos", shaped by language, region and device settings, which TikTok calls weak signals. Personalisation starts when the first likes, comments and replays "initiate an early round of recommendations" ([TikTok Newsroom, 2020](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you); [TikTok Support](https://support.tiktok.com/en/using-tiktok/exploring-videos/how-tiktok-recommends-content)). TikTok's own example of a strong interest signal is finishing a longer video. The current Guidelines say the system "looks at things like what you like, share, comment on, and search for, as well as what's trending" ([TikTok FYF standards](https://www.tiktok.com/safety/en/policies-and-engagement/fyf-standards)). The leaked "TikTok Algo 101" document, which TikTok confirmed as authentic, gives a simplified score: Plike×Vlike + Pcomment×Vcomment + Eplaytime×Vplaytime + Pplay×Vplay. Each term is the predicted probability (or, for playtime, the expected value) of an action times a hidden value weight ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/leaked-tiktok-doc-reveals-its-obvious-secret-to-an-addi-1848166901)). Play and playtime are recorded for every video served, so watching is the densest evidence a new account generates.

Independent audits show that evidence builds up fast. The Wall Street Journal's 2021 test accounts expressed interests only by lingering on or rewatching videos; TikTok identified most of those interests within two hours and some within about 40 minutes, and one account reached a **93% single-topic feed after 224 videos, about 33–36 minutes** of viewing ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2021/07/21/tiktok-recommendation-algorithm-wall-street-journal-guillaume-chaslot/); [Marketing Magazine](https://marketingmagazine.com.my/how-tiktoks-algorithm-works-a-fascinating-and-disturbing-analysis)). Other audits found similar speeds. More than half the feed of Amnesty's manually run 2023 accounts turned to the targeted topic within 3–20 minutes ([Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2023/11/tiktok-risks-pushing-children-towards-harmful-content/)), CCDH's accounts set up as teenagers met targeted content within 2.6–8 minutes ([CCDH](https://counterhate.com/research/deadly-by-design/)), and DCU's male-identified accounts did so within 23 minutes ([DCU](https://www.dcu.ie/instituteofeducation/news/2024/apr/new-research-shows-how-tiktok-and-youtube-shorts-are-bombarding)). A 2026 Northeastern study established each topic interest by watching **200+ videos** ([Northeastern](https://news.northeastern.edu/2026/06/09/tiktok-not-interested-algorithm-control/)). Real-user data point the same way: in the Post's donations, watching just two mental-health videos added 10.7 videos on that topic to the next 500 ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/interactive/2025/tiktok-algorithm-mental-health/)). TikTok's own internal estimate, cited in Kentucky's lawsuit and disputed by the company, is that a viewing habit forms after about 260 videos ([KUNC/NPR](https://www.kunc.org/2024-10-11/lawsuit-documents-reveal-what-tiktok-executives-know-about-apps-effect-on-teenagers)). **Across these sources, a few hundred videos is the unit in which TikTok learns a viewer**, which at a human pace of about 18 seconds a video is an hour to an hour and a half of watching. Three days at 25 minutes a day is about 250 videos, so a typical warm-up's viewing phase already covers it.

These are orders of magnitude, not a schedule. Most audits used sensitive topics and single-minded accounts, which TikTok calls unrepresentative because humans have diverse interests. A 2025 SIGIR replication found earlier TikTok audits poorly reproducible, with findings going stale within about three years ([Mosnar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18140)). Topic matters too: a 2026 audit found misinformation-themed content suppressed while US politics was amplified ([Pecher et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20723v1)). And since January 2026, US users have been on a recommender retrained solely on US data ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/23/jv_announces_tiktok_us_acquisition/)). Even a converged feed keeps exploring: the WSJ's most extreme account still saw 7% off-topic videos. The realistic target is a niche-heavy feed, not a niche-only one.

Which actions teach the algorithm fastest is contested. Boeker and Urman (WWW 2022) ranked **follows first, then likes, then watch rate** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12271)), and Vombatkere et al. (WWW 2024) also found follows the most impactful ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12410v1)). The 2025 SIGIR replication found the opposite: **watching was the strongest signal**, growing with longer or repeated viewing but not beyond about 75% of a video, while likes and follows first triggered exploration of adjacent content before the feed narrowed ([Mosnar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18140)). Northeastern found that tapping "Not interested" suppressed a topic more than simply swiping past it, though suppressed topics came back once the signals stopped ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10690)). No study measures the weight of saves, shares, comments, searches or profile visits. The practical reading is to make watching and explicit actions point the same way: finish, and occasionally rewatch, the niche videos you actually enjoy, swipe quickly past everything else, and reserve "Not interested" for topics you never want to see.

None of this shows that a creator's viewing history affects how far their own videos travel. TikTok's 2020 explainer states that "neither follower count nor whether the account has had previous high-performing videos are direct factors". It names "content you create" as an input to the creator's *own* feed ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)). A popular model says each new video goes first to a small test audience of "200–500" people. That fits ByteDance's prediction-based design, but TikTok has never documented the audience's size or mechanics ([TikTok Discover compilation](https://www.tiktok.com/discover/does-tiktok-show-your-video-to-first)). Leaked "heating" practices also show distribution is not purely algorithmic: staff manually push chosen videos, which one internal playbook put at 1–2% of daily views, although TikTok says such content is about 0.002% of the videos in For You feeds ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2023/01/23/tiktok-admits-its-own-employees-can-decide-what-videos-go-viral/)). **Niche viewing before posting buys a niche-tuned feed and research into the niche's formats, sounds and hooks. It does not demonstrably buy reach.**

## Day by day: three weeks at human pace, first post on day four

The recipe copies the measured light-user baseline, ramps engagement in the order every guide and most real users follow (watch first, then like and follow, then comment, then post), and keeps every action far below reported limits. Each figure carries one of six labels. **Measured** means real-user behaviour observed through data donations, passive panels or account observation. **Official** means TikTok's published rules or statements, which are self-reported where they describe the algorithm. **Derived** means my arithmetic or extrapolation from measured data, right in direction but uncertain in exact value. **Reported** means limits reported by users and vendors and never confirmed by TikTok. **Guide** means a heuristic from a published warm-up guide with no data behind it. **No data** means nobody has measured it, so the figure is a judgement consistent with the rest. Viewing times count scrolling only, not filming or editing. The research behind this report was compiled from search-engine extracts of the cited pages rather than full-text reads, so treat precise-looking figures as approximate.

At these rates, cumulative follows reach roughly 25–75 by day 21. Pew's median is 63 for US adults who never post and 246 for those who do, so that total sits in the ordinary range.

Day one is setup plus listening. Choose interest categories that genuinely match your niche, because TikTok uses them to seed the first feed, and confirm your language and region, which TikTok treats as weak signals ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)). Pick a username you intend to keep, because it can be changed only once every 30 days ([TikTok Support](https://www.tiktok.com/support/faq_detail?id=7096681481912752642)). The photo, nickname and short bio that Creator Academy suggests can go in the same sitting or a few days later; no source shows the timing matters. If you might want Creator Rewards later, stay on a personal account, because business accounts are ineligible ([TikTok Help](https://www.tiktok.com/support/faq_detail?id=7581821550694013452&category=web_account); [@creatoracademy](https://www.tiktok.com/@creatoracademy/video/7654269670391926047)). Skip external links: TikTok says it "may also temporarily stop you from posting links", and a clickable bio link reportedly needs 1,000 followers anyway ([Rocketlink](https://rocketlink.io/blog/tiktok-link-in-bio-requirements)). Expect the first feed to show generic popular videos; two or three niche searches, finishing the good results and swiping past the rest, tilt it within the first session or two. If you are repurposing an existing account, use Settings → Content preferences → Refresh your For You feed, which shows the feed "as if they just signed up for TikTok", instead of opening a second account ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/02/02/tiktok-introduces-a-strike-system-for-violations-tests-a-feature-to-refresh-the-for-you-feed/)).

The texture of each session matters more than its totals. Zannettou's measured distribution sets the pattern: of every ten videos, a natural scroll **swipes two or three away within the first fifth of the clip** (3–6 seconds on a typical 15–30-second video), leaves one or two before the halfway mark and one or two after it, and **watches four or five to the end**. During warm-up, steer the finishes toward niche videos and the early swipes toward everything else, since watching is the one signal every audit found to matter. Finishing everything is unnatural, because 55% of real views end early, and unnecessary, because the signal stops strengthening past about 75% of a clip. Rewatch a standout niche video now and then. TikTok names replays among the first signals it learns from, though nobody publishes how often real users loop.

Spacing matters as much as totals. At about 18 seconds a video and a like on one video in twenty, a natural rhythm is **roughly one like every five or six minutes of scrolling**. Put likes on videos you finished, not on a run of consecutive clips. A typical 7–10-minute open covers 20–30 videos. It might include one like, one profile visit, at most one or two follows, and now and then a search at the start; TikTok says a quarter of users search within 30 seconds of opening. Follow a creator only after visiting their profile and watching two or three of their videos, and never follow in runs. Leave comments one at a time, each specific to the video. Identical or generic comments are the pattern TikTok's rules target: its spam definition covers automation that sends "repetitive content", and comments with "Random text, irrelevant promotions, or links" are sorted lower. Such comments are also reportedly flagged "even well below the count limit" ([creatortribune](https://creatortribune.com/tiktok-like-comment-limit/)). Even an unusually long hour of scrolling at this pace yields about ten likes and a handful of follows. Reported hourly ceilings are roughly 100 likes and 10–30 follows.

Posting begins around day four because by then the account has watched roughly 150–300 videos. That is enough for the feed to show the niche and for you to have studied its formats; no rule or test requires the wait, and posting earlier breaks nothing. Post only original material. TikTok makes "unoriginal or reused material without anything new" ineligible for the For You feed. If an account posts a lot of such content, TikTok can make the whole account "harder to find" ([TikTok Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/safety/en/policies-and-engagement/integrity-authenticity); [Accounts & Features](https://www.tiktok.com/safety/en/policies-and-engagement/accounts-features)). Upload manually in the app for the first week. Even the scheduling vendor OneUp advises this, against its own product ([OneUp](https://help.oneupapp.io/en-us/article/why-are-my-tiktok-videos-getting-0-views-m13bwc/)). Build from one post every other day to three to five a week, then four to seven. Buffer analysed 11.4 million posts from more than 150,000 accounts. Moving from one post a week to 2–5 lifted views per post by up to 17%, and 6–10 a week lifted them by 29%. Median views stayed near 500; what mainly rose was the chance of a breakout ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/how-often-should-you-post-on-tiktok/)). Hootsuite recommends 3–5 posts a week for businesses ([Hootsuite](https://blog.hootsuite.com/how-often-to-post-on-social-media/)). It also quotes an expert who calls "posting new content too often, especially when you first create a TikTok account" spammy ([Hootsuite](https://blog.hootsuite.com/tiktok-shadowban/)). On posting days, keep scrolling at your normal level and reply to comments on your own videos. Give each video a few days before judging it or deleting it ([MonetizedNow](https://monetizednow.com/warm-up-tiktok-account)).

When a video stalls, check instead of guessing. TikTok states that "if a video isn't getting many views, it also doesn't necessarily mean it broke a rule". It shows its decisions in three places: a warning bar in analytics on any post that is ineligible for the For You feed, enforcement actions under Inbox → Account Status, and the Account check tool, which scans the account and its last 30 posts and shows a green tick when nothing is wrong ([TikTok FYF standards](https://www.tiktok.com/safety/en/policies-and-engagement/fyf-standards); [Creator Academy: Account check](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/article/account-check); [Creator Academy: moderation status](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/article/guidelines-moderation-status-and-appeals)). If a "tapping too fast" or "following too frequently" message appears, stop that action for the rest of the day. The recipe deliberately leaves out everything that would turn a person into a pattern TikTok polices: automation or schedulers in the first week, like-for-like swaps or promised follows, bought engagement, reposted clips, and second accounts used to get around a restriction, which TikTok explicitly forbids. No published guidance changes any of this for business versus personal accounts, or for UK versus US users.

## Vendors sell the precision; the evidence backs only the pace

Detailed warm-up schedules come almost entirely from businesses with a stake in warm-up seeming necessary. They include an account seller (MonetizedNow), automation and warm-up services (ReelFarm, Conbersa, Superwarm), and clipping, scheduling and AI-content tools (Ssemble, Cut.Pro, OneUp, SocialKit, Superscale, AI Flow Chat). The rest are a VPN seller (vpntous) and anti-detect browsers built for multi-account operators (Undetectable, Multilogin, GeeLark) ([Conbersa](https://www.conbersa.ai/learn/how-to-warm-up-tiktok-accounts); [ReelFarm](https://reel.farm/guides/how-to-warm-up-tiktok-account); [Undetectable](https://undetectable.io/blog/tiktok-account-warm-up/)). In the searches behind this report, mainstream publishers such as Hootsuite, Later, Buffer, Sprout Social, HubSpot and Metricool published no day-by-day warm-up at all. The vendor schedules disagree on almost every number. Warm-up length runs from 1–2 days (SocialKit) to 1–2 weeks (Undetectable), and daily viewing from 10–15 minutes (Ssemble) to 1–2 hours (Playkit). The first post comes anywhere from day 1, in-app (OneUp), to days 8–10 (MonetizedNow, Conbersa). Superwarm prescribes a minimum of 5–7 days, yet its own TikTok example puts the first post on day 4 ([Superwarm](https://superwarm.co/blog/tiktok-instagram-account-warming-guide)). None cites TikTok documentation or measured data. One anti-detect vendor's 14-day plan (apparently Undetectable's) admits its numbers are not TikTok limits, and SocialKit calls "mandatory waiting periods" and "magic post counts" myths ([SocialKit](https://socialk.it/en/blog/tiktok-account-warmup-guide)). Many of these guides serve operators who run fleets of accounts on shared devices and networks. That risk profile does not transfer to one person on their own phone.

No controlled comparison of warmed and unwarmed accounts exists. The closest material is forum anecdote that points both ways. One BlackHatWorld user saw a dummy video reach about 900 views after warming, where earlier accounts got near zero, but views fell to about 10 after a week of posting ([BlackHatWorld](https://www.blackhatworld.com/seo/method-how-to-warm-up-tiktok-account-properly.1653526/page-2)). Another reported "posting videos like 5 minutes after creating the accounts and got tons of views" ([BlackHatWorld](https://www.blackhatworld.com/seo/how-important-is-it-to-warm-up-a-tiktok-account.1592041/)). Even rigorous audits of TikTok using automated test accounts took months to replicate, and their findings went stale within about three years ([Mosnar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18140)), so vendor "tests" carry little weight.

The guides agree on several points: consume niche content first, ramp up gradually, avoid bursts, post original non-promotional work, skip early links, and post in the app. Each of these lines up with something TikTok demonstrably polices: automation, repetition, fake engagement, unoriginal content and link spam. Where the guides disagree is on exact days, minutes and counts, which is exactly where no evidence exists. ReelFarm's fix for persistent zero views is to start a new account. If the account has actually been restricted, that runs into TikTok's rule that a restricted user "may not create or use another account to get around it" ([TikTok Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/safety/en/policies-and-engagement/integrity-authenticity)); if it has not, a new account solves nothing. A real person should check Account check first.

## Conclusion

"Warm-up" names a pace, not a period. TikTok's enforcement is built to catch machines. It blocks tens of billions of fake likes and follow requests, and during one country's election it stopped hundreds of thousands of spam accounts at signup. A person who scrolls 20–30 minutes a day, likes one video in twenty and follows a few creators after looking at their profiles looks like the median new user, not like the activity those systems hunt. That changes the practical question from "how many days must I wait?" to "is my For You feed mostly my niche, and do I have an original video?", which most people can answer within one to three days. It also shifts diagnosis away from view counts and "200-view jail" toward TikTok's own Account check and Account Status tools. TikTok says it ranks each post without direct regard to follower count or past hits, so the hours a ritual would take are better spent on the video itself.

The unknowns are now clearly defined. Nobody has measured a brand-new account's first week. Nobody has measured how often ordinary viewers comment, save, share, visit profiles, open Explore or watch LIVE. And nobody has tested whether a creator's viewing history affects their reach at all. The audit numbers here also predate the US-only model that American users have been on since January 2026. The vendors with the strongest commercial interest in proving that warm-up works are also the best placed to run a controlled comparison, and none has published one. Until someone does, the defensible strategy is the cheap one: behave like the median user, post original work at a pace you can sustain, and treat any schedule that claims more precision than that as marketing.
